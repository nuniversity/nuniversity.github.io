# Social Enterprise and NGOs

Social enterprises and non-governmental organizations represent distinct yet increasingly interconnected models within the third sector. Understanding their distinctions, overlaps, and strategic choices is essential for leaders navigating the modern non-profit landscape.

## Definitions and Core Distinctions

### Non-Profit Organization (NPO)
- **Primary characteristic**: Non-distribution constraint; surplus revenue must be reinvested in mission
- **Legal forms**: Trust, association, corporation, cooperative
- **Funding mix**: Typically relies on donations, grants, some earned income
- **Ownership**: No private owners; held in trust for public benefit
- **Primary goal**: Mission achievement and organizational sustainability

### Social Enterprise
- **Primary characteristic**: Applies commercial strategies to maximize improvements in financial, social, and environmental well-being
- **Key distinction**: Profit (or surplus) is reinvested in the social mission, not distributed to owners, but the model explicitly blends business principles with social goals
- **Legal forms**: Benefit corporation (B-Corp), low-profit limited liability company (L3C), cooperative, social cooperative, community interest company, traditional corporation with social mission
- **Funding mix**: Explicitly includes revenue-generating activities as primary or significant income source
- **Primary goal**: Social value creation + financial sustainability through earned income

### Non-Governmental Organization (NGO)
- **Primary characteristic**: Not part of government; can be non-profit or for-profit; focuses on humanitarian, social, environmental, or developmental issues
- **Scope**: Can operate local, regional, national, or internationally
- **Classification by orientation**: Charity, service, participatory, empowerment, professional/trade association
- **Classification by level**: Community-based, city-wide, state, national, international (INGO)
- **Funding mix**: Highly variable; can include government funding, donations, earned income, membership fees

## Key Comparison Table

| Dimension | Non-Profit Organization | Social Enterprise | NGO |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Profit Distribution** | Reinvested in mission; cannot distribute to owners | Reinvested in mission; surplus may have limited distribution rules | Varies by legal structure; NPO NGOS cannot distribute profits |
| **Primary Revenue Model** | Donations, grants, some fees | Explicitly earned income + possibly donations | Highly variable; donations, grants, government funding, fees |
| **Legal Focus** | Mission non-distribution constraint | Triple bottom line (financial, social, environmental) | independence from government; may have charitable status |
| **Market Orientation** | Mission-first; market used when supportive | Market-first with social/environmental embed | Mission/issue-first; market used as tool |
| **Accountability Primary** | Donors, regulators, beneficiaries | Customers, beneficiaries, investors, regulators | Donors, government funders, beneficiaries, public |
| **Typical Legal Structure** | Corporation, trust, association | B-Corp, L3C, cooperative, benefit corp | Trust, association, corporation, foundation |
| **Philosophy** | "Doing good by doing good" | "Doing good by doing business" | "Doing good independently of government" |

## Social Enterprise Business Models

### 1. Trading Enterprises
- **Concept**: Sell goods/services related to mission; profits fund mission activities
- **Examples**: Fair trade product sales, social retail shops, community enterprises
- **Scale**: Ranges from small worker cooperatives (e.g., John Lewis Partnership in UK) to large employer-owned businesses
- **Key success factors**: Market demand for product; clear connection between purchase and mission impact; sustainable pricing

### 2. Service Providers
- **Concept**: Charge fees for services while ensuring access for underserved populations
- **Examples**: Sliding-scale healthcare clinics, fee-based training/certification, community-based tourism
- **Revenue approach**: Tiered pricing; subsidies for low-income clients; cross-subsidization
- **Key success factors**: Balanced cost structure; clear value proposition; effective subsidy management

### 3. Financial Institutions
- **Concept**: Provide financial services to underserved populations; sustain through fees + optional philanthropy
- **Examples**: Credit unions, microcredit organizations, cooperative banks, revolving loan funds
- **Ownership**: Membership-owned; profits reinvested or returned to members as reduced fees
- **Key success factors**: Financial literacy; appropriate risk management; local context understanding

### 4. Hybrid Models (Most Common)
- **Concept**: Combination of earned income and contributed income; explicit mission/finance balance
- **Examples**: Universities (tuition + endowments + donations), hospitals (services + insurance + donations), museums (admission + grants + membership)
- **Revenue mix**: Typically 40-60% earned income, 40-60% contributed income (varies widely)
- **Key success factors**: Sophisticated financial management; clear stakeholder communication; integrated reporting

## NGO Classification and Levels of Operation

### By Orientation (Type of Activities)

| Orientation | Description | Typical Activities |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Charity** | Top-down resource transfer to disadvantaged | Disaster relief, material assistance, top-down service delivery |
| **Service** | Provide healthcare, education, other services | Clinics, schools, training programs, direct service delivery |
| **Participatory** | Self-help with local involvement | Community projects, self-help groups, local resource contribution |
| **Empowerment** | Help poor understand power structures; NGOs as facilitators | Community organizing, leadership development, rights education |
| **Professional/Trade** | Voluntary association of people with common interests | Professional associations, trade unions, cultural organizations |

### By Level of Operation

| Level | Description | Typical Characteristics |
|-------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Community-based** (CBO) | Local initiatives; popular consciousness-raising | Urban poor empowerment; right-to-services advocacy; local service provision |
| **City-wide** | Chambers of commerce, coalitions, community orgs | Business advocacy; ethnic/educational group coalitions; city organizing |
| **State** | State-level organizations, associations, groups | National/international federation guidance; resource sharing; coordinated action |
| **National** | Organizations existing in one country only | YMCA/YWCA; professional associations; assist local NGOs; some branches |
| **International** (INGO) | Secular or religious agencies operating globally | Save the Children, Red Cross; fund/local NGOs; implement direct projects |

## Legal Forms for Social Enterprises

### Benefit Corporation (B-Corp)
- **Legal status**: For-profit corporation with social/environmental mission mandated in governing documents
- **Key requirement**: Directors must consider impact on workers, community, environment (not just shareholders)
- **Verification**: B Lab certification (optional but widely recognized); legal requirement varies by jurisdiction
- **Transparency**: Annual benefit report assessing social/environmental performance
- **Jurisdictions**: US (30+ states), Europe, Asia, Latin America (including Brazil via specific enabling legislation)

### Low-Profit Limited Liability Company (L3C)
- **Legal status**: Hybrid legal form; main objective is socially beneficial goals
- **Requirements (US states that authorize)**:
  1. Operate for charitable or educational purposes
  2. Not the production of income as primary purpose
  3. Not the fulfillment of a political or legislative agenda
- **IRS treatment**: Treated as partnership for tax purposes; can receive program-related investments from private foundations
- **Best for**: Organizations wanting for-profit flexibility with social mission focus

### Social Cooperative
- **Legal status**: Democratic member-owned enterprise with social mission
- **Key characteristics**: One member, one vote; common ownership; profit reinvested for members' benefit or community good
- **Models**: Work cooperatives; consumer cooperatives; producer cooperatives
- **Global presence**: Strong in Europe (especially Southern Europe); growing in other regions
- **Brazil context**: Regulated under specific cooperative legislation; can serve social missions with member benefit

### Community Interest Company (CIC) - UK Model
- **Legal status**: UK-specific; asset lock ensures assets used for community benefit
- **Key features**: Asset lock (cannot distribute assets on winding up); "community interest statement"; regulator (CIC regulator)
- **Asset lock**: On winding up, assets must transfer to another asset-locked organization
- **Profit distribution**: Dividends capped; excess profit must be reinstated in community benefit
- **Not directly applicable in Brazil** but informs understanding of legal forms with mission lock

### Traditional Non-Profit with Earned Income
- **Legal status**: Standard NPO form (corporation, trust, association)
- **Approach**: Engage in revenue-generating activities related to mission; profits reinvested in NPO structure
- **Constraints**: Unrelated business income tax (UBIT) may apply; must maintain non-distribution constraint
- **Best for**: Existing non-profits wanting to diversify revenue without changing legal structure

## Strategic Choices: When to Choose Which Model

### Choose NPO Model When:
- Mission requires tax-exempt status for donor attractiveness
- Primary funding comes from grants/donations
- Organization wants to avoid commercial pressures on mission
- Regulatory simplicity is priority
- Example: Humanitarian relief organization, membership association

### Choose Social Enterprise Model When:
- Earned income can significantly fund mission (goal: reduce donor dependence)
- Market exists for goods/services aligned with mission
- Organization wants commercial credibility and sustainability
- Long-term financial independence is strategic goal
- Example: Microcredit organization, social retail, community enterprise

### Choose NGO Model When:
- International or national advocacy is primary goal
- Government funding or partnerships are essential
- Issue requires policy change or systemic reform
- Organization needs to operate across borders
- Example: International development NGO, human rights organization, environmental advocacy NGO

### Choose Hybrid Model When:
- Organization wants balance of mission focus and financial sustainability
- Diversified funding strategy is strategic goal
- Organization is transitioning from donor-dependent to more self-sustaining
- Example: University, hospital, museum, symphony orchestra

## Academic and Practitioner Literature

### Key Academic Contributions

1. **Gregory Dees and Beth Anderson**: "Defined the innovation that differentiates the social enterprise from the traditional non-profit actor" - identified funding strategy transition as key differentiator

2. **EMES Network** (European Research Network on Social Economy and Social Enterprise): 
   - Identified 5+ million social economy organizations in Europe
   - Established language and frameworks still used globally
   - Spread from Italy's "red belt" (7,000 worker/social co-operatives) to UK and Europe

3. **Harvard, Stanford, Princeton universities**: 
   - Built on Ashoka's work to develop social entrepreneurship field
   - Contributed project initiatives and publications
   - Influenced pedagogy and research agendas

4. **Robert DeSousa and others**: 
   - Social enterprise emergence as result of state and market failure
   - Different emphasis: UK emphasizes market failure; US emphasizes state failure

5. **Critical traditions**: 
   - **Institutional theory**: Models legitimized and widely accepted drive adoption
   - **Critical pedagogy** (Paulo Freire influence): Examines power dynamics in social change work
   - **Marxism and institutional theory**: Inform social economy debates

### Key Practitioner Frameworks

1. **The Social Enterprise Alliance (SEA)**:
   - Identified 4 factors causing non-profit-to-social-enterprise transition:
     - Increase in non-profit operating costs
     - Decline in government and public philanthropic support
     - Increased competition due to growth in charitable sector
     - Expansion in demand for non-profit provided services
   - 2012 survey data: Donations 12% of income; largest source (43%) from revenue-generating activities

2. **Social Impact Measurement**:
   - SROI (Social Return on Investment) methodology
   - Logic Models and Theories of Change
   - Impact investing frameworks
   - Stakeholder triangulation approaches

3. **Legal Form Transition Guides**:
   - Step-by-step conversion processes
   - Risk assessment matrices
   - Stakeholder communication strategies

## Brazil-Specific Social Enterprise Landscape

### Legal Framework
- **Lei nº 13.019/2014 (Third Sector Law)**: Primary legislation governing non-profits; establishes OSCIP, Associação, Fundação forms
- **Social enterprise specific**: No dedicated "social enterprise law"; models operate under general NPO framework or commercial law
- **Benefit Corporation**: Brazil has draft legislation; not yet widely adopted; organizations typically use B-Corp certification from B Lab or operate as traditional NPOs with earned income

### Operating Models in Practice
1. **NGO with earned income programs**: Most common; NGOs run income-generating activities (workshops, training, social products) with profits reinvested
2. **OSCIP with commercial arms**: OSCIPs (most prestigious NPO type) can establish separate commercial entities for income generation
3. **Co-operatives with social mission**: Growing sector; especially in agriculture, handicrafts, services; regulated under cooperative law with social mission provisions
4. **Informal social enterprises**: Community-based initiatives operating without formal legal structure; common in favelas and peripheral urban areas

### Key Brazilian Organizations and Networks
- **Articulação Brasileira de ONGs (ABONG)**: Umbrella organization; capacity building; sector advocacy
- **Observatório do Terceiro Setor**: Research, statistics, policy analysis; sector knowledge base
- **Articulação Movimento Nacional de Direitos Humanos**: Human rights focus; legal support; monitoring
- **Sebrae Social**: Sebrae (small business support agency) social entrepreneurship programs
- **Acumen Brazil**: Impact investing organization; invests in social enterprises; builds ecosystem

### Challenges Specific to Brazil
- **Bureaucracy**: CNPJ registration; annual reporting; tax compliance can be complex and time-consuming
- **Funding volatility**: Government funding cuts (2016-2022 economic crisis); donor uncertainty
- **Governance**: Founder's syndrome well-documented; board engagement often lacking
- **Legal uncertainty**: No clear social enterprise specific legislation; organizations must navigate between NPO and commercial frameworks
- **Market limitations**: Formal market for social impact products/services still developing; consumer awareness growing but not mature

### Opportunities in Brazil
- **Growing awareness**: Increased recognition of third sector's role; more corporate social responsibility initiatives
- **Impact investing**: Growing but still early-stage; organizations like Acumen, local impact funds emerging
- **Policy attention**: Third sector receiving more governmental attention; policy reforms under discussion
- **Digital transformation**: Mobile technology enabling new service delivery and revenue models
- **Youth engagement**: New generation of social entrepreneurs; more willing to blend business and mission
