# Measuring Non-Profit Impact

Effective impact measurement is essential for demonstrating value, improving operations, securing funding, and fulfilling mission accountability. This lesson covers comprehensive impact assessment frameworks and methodologies.

## Impact Assessment Frameworks

### 1. Logic Models

#### Core Components
A Logic Model is a visual representation of the relationships among resources, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact. The standard framework includes:

| Component | Description | Example (Education Non-Profit) |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| **Resources** (Inputs) | Assets invested in the program; funding, staff, volunteers, materials, facilities, technology | $250K annual budget; 3 full-time staff; 15 volunteers; community center space; curriculum materials; laptops |
| **Activities** | What the program does with resources; services delivered; events held | Weekly tutoring sessions (2 hrs each); monthly parent workshops; summer enrichment camps; homework help sessions |
| **Outputs** | Direct products/services delivered; measurable counts | 150 students served; 20 parent workshops held; 30 students attended summer camp; 450 homework help sessions conducted |
| **Outcomes** | Changes in knowledge, skills, attitudes, behavior resulting from outputs | 80% of students improve reading level by at least 1 grade; 70% of parents increase home reading frequency; 60% of summer camp students maintain or improve school-year math skills |
| **Impact** | Longer-term, broader changes contributed to by the program (within logic's boundary) | Improved high school graduation rates in the community; reduced achievement gap; increased community literacy rates; enhanced workforce preparation |

#### Logic Model Quality Criteria
- **Clarity**: Each component clearly defined; no ambiguity
- **Sequentiality**: Logical flow from resources to impact; each step connected to next
- **Measurability**: Outputs, outcomes, and impact can be measured (or have reasonable proxies)
- **Attribution**: Causal link between program and outcomes can be supported (or limitations honestly stated)
- **Stakeholder-Informed**: Developed with input from those served; reflects community reality

#### Common Logic Model Pitfalls
- **Output-outcome confusion**: Reporting counts (outputs) as changes (outcomes); e.g., "500 workshops held" vs. "participants gained knowledge"
- **Overly broad impact**: Claiming credit for long-term societal changes impossible to attribute to single program; e.g., "we reduced city-wide poverty" when program serves 50 families
- **Unrealistic measurement**: Measuring things that can't actually be measured with available resources; e.g., surveys too long; data collection burden causes respondent fatigue
- **Missing reverse causality**: Not considering that participants may have certain characteristics before program; e.g., families who self-select into program may already be more motivated

### 2. Theory of Change

#### Core Concept
Theory of Change (TOC) is a comprehensive description and illustration of how and why a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context. It addresses the "missing middle" between activities and outcomes.

#### TOC vs. Logic Model Comparison

| Aspect | Logic Model | Theory of Change |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| **Focus** | Program-level; shorter-term; linear pathway | Program/initiative-level; broader; causal pathways; context-dependent |
| **Time Horizon** | Often 1-3 years; program duration | Often 3-10+ years; systemic change |
| **Causality** | Assumes if-then relationships; simpler chain | Explicitly maps assumptions; explores multiple pathways; context factors |
| **Complexity** | Simpler; good for program reporting | More complex; better for systemic change, advocacy, multi-year initiatives |
| **Development Time** | Can be developed relatively quickly | Requires extensive stakeholder consultation; research; assumption testing |
| **Best Use** | Program reporting; grant reporting; operational monitoring | Strategy development; policy advocacy; multi-year initiatives; cross-sector partnerships; evaluation design |

#### Theory of Change Components
- **Long-term goal**: The ultimate change the initiative seeks to contribute to
- **Preconditions**: Intermediate outcomes that must occur for long-term goal to be achieved; sequenced logically
- **Causal links**: Explicit "if-then" statements connecting each level; supported by evidence or justified reasoning
- **Assumptions**: External factors that must hold true; risks; context dependencies; made explicit and testable
- **Activities/outputs**: What the initiative does; mapped to how they contribute to each precondition
- **Context factors**: External conditions that enhance or hinder change; not controlled by initiative but must be considered

#### Developing a Theory of Change: Process
1. **Define long-term goal**: Articulate the ultimate change; be specific; ground in stakeholder vision; ensure it's achievable within initiative scope
2. **Identify preconditions**: Work backwards from goal; what must be true; sequence logically; typically 3-6 preconditions at each level
3. **Map causal links**: For each "if-then"; support with evidence (research, data, precedents) or justify reasoning; be explicit
4. **Identify assumptions**: For each link and the overall pathway; distinguish between controllable and uncontrollable; test where possible
5. **Map activities**: Connect program activities to each causal link; illustrate how inputs lead to outputs which lead to outcomes
6. **Validate with stakeholders**: Present to those served; staff; funders; experts; gather feedback; revise as needed
7. **Document and use**: Keep as living document; use for evaluation design; resource allocation; strategy decisions; communicate to stakeholders

### 3. Social Return on Investment (SROI)

#### Core Concept
SROI is a methodology for measuring and communicating a broader concept of value that incorporates social, environmental, and economic outcomes. It calculates the "social return" per unit of investment.

#### SROI Calculation Steps

| Step | Description | Key Considerations |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **1. Establish Scope** | Define what's included/excluded in the analysis; boundaries affect result significantly | Stakeholder consultation; realistic scope; document what's excluded and why |
| **2. Map Outcomes** | Identify all changed outcomes for stakeholders; consult stakeholders; avoid omission | Mixed methods; interviews; surveys; focus groups; document; create outcome map |
| **3. Evidence Collection** | Gather data on causality; distinguish between contribution and attribution; be transparent about what can be claimed | Most difficult step; use best available data; acknowledge limitations; triangulate multiple sources |
| **4. Monetize Outcomes** | Assign monetary value to identified changes; use market values; distributional weights; be transparent about methodology | Avoid over-monetization; use conservative estimates; range analysis; sensitivity testing |
| **5. Calculate Impact** | (Net present value of benefits ÷ Invested capital) × 100; ratio > 1:1 = social value created | Discount rate selection (typically 3-7%); time horizon; net present value calculation; report ratio |
| **6. Sensitivity Analysis** | Test key assumptions; report range rather than single point; identify most influential variables | One-factor-at-a-time; scenario analysis; Monte Carlo if resources allow; focus on assumptions with most impact |

#### SROI Ratio Interpretation
- **SROI > 1:1**: Social value created; for every $1 invested, $1+ of social value generated
- **SROI = 1:1**: Break-even; social value equals investment (not counting intangibles; distribution effects)
- **SROI < 1:1**: Social value less than investment; may still be worthwhile for other reasons (mission, ethics, partial contribution)
- **Typical Ranges by Sector**:
  - **Education**: 2:1 to 10:1 (varies widely by level, population, metrics)
  - **Health**: 3:1 to 15:1 (depends on intervention; preventive care often higher; treatment variable)
  - **Human Services**: 1:1 to 5:1 (housing, food security, employment services variable)
  - **Environment**: 5:1 to 20:1+ (ecosystem services; climate mitigation often very high; biodiversity variable)

#### SROI Example (Hypothetical)
| Component | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **Investment** | $500,000 over 3 years |
| **Identified Benefits**: | |
| - Increased earnings (participants) | $300,000 (present value) |
| - Reduced social service costs | $100,000 (present value) |
| - Tax revenue increases | $50,000 (present value) |
| - Volunteered time value | $50,000 (present value) |
| **Total Benefits** | $500,000 (present value) |
| **SROI Ratio** | $500,000 ÷ $500,000 = 1:1 |
| **Interpretation** | Social value equals investment; break-even; specific context matters; may still be worthwhile for mission reasons |

#### SROI Best Practices
- **Involve stakeholders**: In outcome identification; monetization choices; validation; not extractive; compensative where possible
- **Be conservative**: In monetization; avoid inflating; use ranges; acknowledge uncertainty; transparency is credibility
- **Use multiple methods**: SROI complemented by qualitative; mixed methods strengthen credibility; different perspectives; triangulation
- **Report comprehensively**: Not just ratio; include; what's included/excluded; assumptions; sensitivity analysis; stakeholder perspectives; limitations; timeline; context
- **Iterate and improve**: First SROI is learning; subsequent versions improve; refine assumptions; add stakeholders; update with new data

## Mixed Methods Impact Assessment

### Quantitative + Qualitative Integration

| Integration Type | Description | When to Use |
|-----------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Triangulation** | Using multiple data sources to confirm findings; convergence increases credibility; e.g., survey data + interview data show same outcome | When seeking confirmation; establishing what happened; baseline assessment |
| **Complementarity** | Quantitative shows scale/magnitude; qualitative shows depth/context/meaning; together provide complete picture | When need both "how many/much" and "how it felt/what it meant"; most common approach |
| **Development** | Quantitative informs qualitative questions; qualitative explains/illustrates quantitative results; iterative | When quantitative reveals patterns that need explanation; or qualitative reveals rich data that needs statistical validation |
| **Emancipatory** | Giving voice to marginalized stakeholders; action-oriented; change-oriented; research "with" not "on" | When social justice; power imbalance; community-led evaluation; priority for stakeholder voice |

#### Mixed Methods Design Examples

**Design A: Explanatory Sequential**
1. Collect + analyze quantitative data (survey of 500 beneficiaries)
2. Use results to inform qualitative data collection (interviews with 20 selected participants to explain quantitative patterns)
3. Integrate results: quantitative patterns explained by qualitative insights; joint display; overall assessment

**Design A: Embedded Quantitative + Qualitative**
1. Primary focus: quantitative experiment (randomized control trial with 300 participants)
2. Embedded: qualitative case studies within the RCT sites; weekly observations; participant journals
3. Integrate: quantitative treatment+effects; qualitative mechanisms; combined display; comprehensive understanding

**Design A: Concurrent Triangulation**
1. Collect quantitative + qualitative data simultaneously (survey + focus groups with 30 participants; survey with 300 participants)
2. Integrate: compare; contrast; merge; connect; diverge; initiate; extend; challenge findings; overall assessment; validity checks

### 2. Stakeholder Analysis and Feedback Mechanisms

#### Stakeholder Identification
| Stakeholder Group | What They Want to Know | Why They Matter |
|------------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Beneficiaries** | Their story reflected; voice heard; impact on their lives; program relevance | Direct; primary; essential; often marginalized; must have meaningful participation |
| **Donors/Funders** | Proof of investment value; impact stories; return on investment; financial stewardship | Critical for sustainability; resource control; strategic decisions; often largest funder |
| **Board** | Organizational health; strategic insights; risk assessment; compliance | Governance; oversight; fiduciary responsibility; decision-making; future direction |
| **Staff/Volunteers** | Job satisfaction; program effectiveness; resource adequacy; voice in decisions | Operational; implementation; retention; culture; continuous improvement |
| **Community/Partners** | Collaboration opportunities; systemic change contribution; reputation; resource access | Broader ecosystem; policy influence; sustainability; shared goals; complementary work |
| **Regulators** | Compliance; proper use of funds; reporting accuracy | Legal; risk management; tax status; reputation; funding continuation |

#### Feedback Mechanisms
| Mechanism | Purpose | Frequency | Key Considerations |
|-----------|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Beneficiary Surveys** | Direct feedback on program quality; relevance; satisfaction; improvement ideas | Quarterly/Annually; at exit; needs-based | Accessible language; anonymous option; incentive; culturally appropriate; non-burdensome; action on results |
| **Focus Groups** | Deep dive; group dynamics; normative issues; detailed insights; program improvement | Semi-annually; program milestones; 6-12 participants; facilitator trained; safe space; refreshments; transcription; analysis; results shared | Ground rules; ground rules; dominant participant management; sensitive topics; ethical clearance if needed |
| **Staff/Volunteer Check-ins** | Operational feedback; resource needs; program adaptation; morale | Monthly; 1:1 meetings; anonymous suggestion box; team meetings | Safe space; no retaliation; action on feedback; protected time; confidentiality where promised |
| **Partner Interviews** | Collaboration assessment; systemic change contribution; resource alignment; future partnership | Annually; partnership renewal; 15-30 minute structured interview; reciprocal; notes; follow-up; results shared | Maintain relationships; mutual benefit; confidentiality; shared goals; future planning |
| **Board Retreats** | Strategic feedback; governance health; CEO evaluation; resource allocation; direction | Annually; off-site; facilitated; full day or half-day; pre-read materials; clear agenda; documented decisions; follow-up | Off-site change; retreat facilitator; clear objectives; follow-up commitments; work-life balance consideration |

### 3. Common Impact Measurement Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

| Pitfall | Description | Prevention |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| **Impact Washing** | Overstating/improperly claiming impact; using misleading metrics; selective reporting | Rigorous evidence standards; transparent uncertainty; conservative estimates; third-party validation where possible; honest reporting of limitations |
| **Outcome Confusion** | Confusing outputs (counts) with outcomes (changes); e.g., reporting "500 attended" as "500 learned" | Clear definitions; logic modeling; theory of change; outcome indicators with baseline and target; training for all staff |
| **Attribution Error** | Claiming program caused outcomes that external factors also influenced; over-claiming | Contribution analysis; realistic boundaries; stakeholder consultation; counterfactual thinking; most significant change technique; mixed methods |
| **Data Collection Burden** | Too much data collected; not useful data; respondent fatigue; low response rates; high cost per data point | Start simple; focus on what decisions drive; mixed methods; light-touch metrics; technology-enabled; respondent burden assessment; iterative refinement |
| **Benefit Overstatement** | Inflating beneficiary counts; exaggerating impact; selective reporting | Independent verification; conservative estimates; baseline data; transparency about methodology; audit trail; stakeholder review |
| **One-Size-Fits-All** | Borrowing metrics without adaptation; using indicators developed for different context; cultural mismatch | Context-specific metric development; stakeholder engagement; pilot testing; adaptation; localization; pilot then scale |
| **Short-Termism** | Measuring only immediate outputs; ignoring longer-term outcomes; missing sustainability | Theory of change; long-term indicators; follow-up assessments; mixed timeline; periodic (3-year, 5-year) check-ins; longitudinal components |
