## Course Description

Classical mechanics covering kinematics, dynamics, energy, momentum, rotational motion, and oscillations. This course connects fundamental physics principles to **real-world applications in engineering, sports, transportation, and space exploration**. Students will learn through **PhET simulations, virtual experiments, and narrative-driven problem solving**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Describe motion using kinematic equations
2. Apply Newton's laws to solve force problems
3. Use energy conservation principles
4. Analyze momentum and collisions
5. Understand rotational motion and angular momentum

## Real-World Examples

### Transportation

**Example 1: Car Crash Analysis**
When a car hits a wall at 60 mph and stops in 0.1 seconds, the deceleration is:
```
a = Δv/Δt = (0 - 27 m/s) / 0.1 s = -270 m/s²
```
That's about 27g's - which is why airbags are essential!

**Example 2: Train Braking Distance**
A train moving at 100 km/h needs about 1 km to stop. Understanding momentum helps engineers design better braking systems.

### Sports

**Example 3: Basketball Free Throw**
A basketball player shoots at 45° angle with initial velocity 8 m/s. Using projectile motion:
- Horizontal distance: 6.5 meters
- Maximum height: 1.6 meters
- Time in air: 1.15 seconds

**Example 4: Tennis Ball Spin**
Topspin on a tennis ball creates a Magnus effect, causing the ball to dip faster. This is why players can hit the ball harder and still keep it in court.

### Space Exploration

**Example 5: International Space Station**
The ISS orbits at 28,000 km/h at 400 km altitude. It's in free fall - astronauts float because they're falling around the Earth, not because there's no gravity.

**Example 6: Mars Rover Landing**
The Mars rover must land in a very specific area. Understanding projectile motion and atmospheric drag helps engineers design the landing trajectory.

## Story: The Falling Apple

> **Hook**: "Did Isaac Newton really get hit by an apple? Probably not. But the story captures something important about how physics works."

> **Story**: In 1666, during the Great Plague, Newton retreated to his family farm. Legend has it that while sitting in the garden, he saw an apple fall from a tree and wondered: does the same force that pulls the apple to the ground also keep the Moon in orbit? This insight led to the law of universal gravitation and his masterpiece, the Principia.

> **Connection**: Every time you see a satellite in the sky, you're seeing Newton's insight in action. The same force that pulls you down keeps them up - because they're moving fast enough to fall around the Earth.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Kinematics Introduction

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "When you look at a speedometer, you're seeing physics in action - the derivative of position with respect to time."

**Story**: "In 1969, Apollo 11 was descending to the Moon's surface. The computer was calculating velocity and acceleration in real-time. When Neil Armstrong saw they were heading for a boulder field, he took manual control and flew the lunar module to a safe spot - using the same kinematic equations you'll learn here."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define position, velocity, and acceleration
- Use kinematic equations for constant acceleration
- Analyze motion graphs
- Solve free-fall problems

**Content Outline:**

#### 1. Position and Displacement (12 min)
- Reference frames
- Position as a function of time
- Displacement vs. distance

#### 2. Velocity (12 min)
- Average vs. instantaneous velocity
- Velocity-time graphs
- Speed vs. velocity

#### 3. Acceleration (12 min)
- Definition of acceleration
- Acceleration-time graphs
- Constant acceleration

#### 4. Kinematic Equations (12 min)
- The four equations
- When to use each equation
- Free-fall problems

#### 5. Interactive Elements (12 min)
- **PhET Simulation: Forces and Motion**
- **Graph Analyzer**: Match position, velocity, acceleration graphs
- **Free-Fall Calculator**: Drop objects from different heights

**Interactive Components:**
```math
v = v_0 + at
```
```math
x = x_0 + v_0t + \frac{1}{2}at^2
```
```math
v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)
```

**Real-World Application**: Car crash analysis - calculating impact force

**Practice Problems:**
1. A car accelerates from rest at 3 m/s² for 10 seconds. What is its final velocity?
2. A ball is thrown upward with 20 m/s. How high does it go?
3. A car traveling at 30 m/s brakes at -6 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?
4. How long does it take for a rock to fall 100 meters?
5. A ball is dropped from 45 meters. What is its velocity just before hitting the ground?

### Lesson 02: Vectors in Physics

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook**: "Vectors are the language of physics. They tell us not just how much, but also which direction."

**Story**: "Navigation apps use vectors every day. When Google Maps tells you to walk 200 meters northeast, it's giving you a vector - both magnitude (200 m) and direction (northeast). The app is solving vector addition problems in real-time."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Represent vectors graphically
- Add and subtract vectors
- Resolve vectors into components
- Use vector addition to solve problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Navigation**: GPS, flight paths
- **Engineering**: Force analysis
- **Sports**: Projectile motion, team strategies
- **Physics**: Electric and magnetic fields

**Practice Problems:**
1. A plane flies 100 km east, then 50 km north. What is its displacement?
2. Two forces of 10 N and 15 N act at 90°. Find the resultant.
3. A projectile is launched at 30° with 40 m/s. Find horizontal and vertical components.
4. A boat crosses a river at 5 m/s while the current flows at 3 m/s. What is the boat's velocity relative to shore?
5. Add vectors: A = (3, 4), B = (-1, 2)

### Lesson 03: Newton's Laws

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Newton's laws are the foundation of classical mechanics. They explain everything from why you feel heavier in an elevator to how rockets escape Earth's gravity."

**Story**: "When astronauts on the ISS appear to float, it's not because there's no gravity - at 400 km altitude, gravity is still 90% as strong as on the surface. They float because they're in free fall, constantly falling around the Earth. Newton's laws explain this beautiful dance."

**Learning Objectives:**
- State and apply Newton's three laws
- Draw and analyze free-body diagrams
- Solve problems involving friction
- Apply Newton's laws to real-world scenarios

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Transportation**: Car safety, seatbelts, airbags
- **Space**: Rocket launches, orbital mechanics
- **Sports**: Action-reaction in swimming, walking
- **Engineering**: Structural analysis

**Practice Problems:**
1. A 5 kg box is pulled with 20 N force at 30°. What is the acceleration?
2. Two people push a car: one with 200 N east, another with 150 N north. What is the net force?
3. A 10 kg block sits on a rough surface (μ = 0.3). What force is needed to move it?
4. Calculate the normal force on a 70 kg person in an elevator accelerating upward at 2 m/s².
5. A rocket has mass 1000 kg and engines produce 15,000 N of thrust. What is the acceleration?

### Lesson 04: Forces in Equilibrium

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "When you stand still, you're in equilibrium. Every force acting on you is perfectly balanced."

**Story**: "The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 because engineers didn't understand dynamic equilibrium. Wind caused the bridge to oscillate until it tore itself apart. Today, engineers use the principles you'll learn here to prevent such disasters."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define equilibrium
- Apply equilibrium conditions
- Solve equilibrium problems
- Understand tension, normal force, and friction

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Civil Engineering**: Building design, bridge construction
- **Architecture**: Structural integrity
- **Everyday**: Standing, sitting, carrying objects
- **Transportation**: Vehicle stability

**Practice Problems:**
1. A sign hangs from two cables at 30° angles. If the sign weighs 200 N, what is the tension in each cable?
2. A 50 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator. What does the scale read if the elevator is stationary?
3. A ladder leans against a wall at 60°. What friction force prevents it from slipping?
4. Three forces act on an object: 10 N east, 10 N north, and 10√2 N at 225°. Is the object in equilibrium?
5. A traffic light hangs from two cables. What is the tension in each cable?

### Lesson 05: Work and Energy

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Energy is the currency of the universe. Understanding it helps us design better cars, build more efficient buildings, and even understand how our bodies work."

**Story**: "In 1843, James Joule demonstrated that mechanical work could be converted to heat. He used a falling weight to stir water and measured the temperature rise. This experiment proved that energy is conserved - it just changes form. The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define work and calculate it
- Understand kinetic and potential energy
- Apply the work-energy theorem
- Use conservation of energy

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Engineering**: Energy efficiency, renewable energy
- **Sports**: Optimizing athletic performance
- **Transportation**: Fuel efficiency, regenerative braking
- **Everyday**: Energy consumption, conservation

**Practice Problems:**
1. How much work is done lifting a 10 kg box 2 meters?
2. A 2 kg ball is thrown at 10 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?
3. A roller coaster starts from rest at 50 m height. What is its speed at the bottom?
4. A car brakes from 30 m/s to rest. How much work do the brakes do?
5. A pendulum is released from 30°. What is its speed at the bottom?

### Lesson 06: Momentum and Collisions

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Momentum is mass in motion. It's why a bowling ball is harder to stop than a tennis ball, even at the same speed."

**Story**: "In 1952, the US Navy tested nuclear-powered submarines. They needed to understand how momentum works underwater. The same principles help us design safer cars, better sports equipment, and even understand how galaxies collide."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define momentum and impulse
- Apply conservation of momentum
- Analyze elastic and inelastic collisions
- Solve real-world collision problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Automotive**: Car safety, crumple zones
- **Sports**: collisions, equipment design
- **Space**: Rocket propulsion
- **Engineering**: Impact analysis

**Practice Problems:**
1. A 1000 kg car traveling at 20 m/s hits a wall and stops in 0.1 s. What is the average force?
2. Two billiard balls collide: ball 1 (0.5 kg) at 4 m/s hits ball 2 (0.5 kg) at rest. If they stick together, what is their final velocity?
3. A 70 kg person jumps from a 3 m height. What impulse does the ground exert to stop them?
4. A rocket expels gas at 3000 m/s. If the rocket has mass 1000 kg, what is the initial acceleration?
5. A hockey puck (0.2 kg) traveling at 10 m/s is struck by a stick. If the puck leaves at 15 m/s in the opposite direction, what impulse was applied?

### Lesson 07: Rotational Motion

**Duration:** 75 minutes

**Hook**: "Why does a figure skater spin faster when she pulls her arms in? Angular momentum - the rotational equivalent of linear momentum."

**Story**: "In 1687, Newton showed that the same laws that govern falling apples also govern spinning tops. This insight led to the development of gyroscopes, which now guide aircraft, spacecraft, and even your smartphone's orientation sensor."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define angular position, velocity, and acceleration
- Calculate torque and moment of inertia
- Apply rotational kinematic equations
- Understand angular momentum conservation

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Transportation**: Vehicle dynamics, braking
- **Spacecraft**: Attitude control, momentum wheels
- **Sports**: Diving, gymnastics, figure skating
- **Engineering**: Rotating machinery, turbines

**Practice Problems:**
1. A wheel accelerates from rest to 100 rpm in 5 seconds. What is its angular acceleration?
2. A force of 20 N is applied at 0.5 m from a pivot. What is the torque?
3. A disk (I = 0.5 kg·m²) rotates at 10 rad/s. What is its angular momentum?
4. A figure skater with I = 4 kg·m² spins at 2 rev/s. If she pulls her arms in and I becomes 2 kg·m², what is her new spin rate?
5. A merry-go-round (I = 500 kg·m²) rotates at 1 rev/s. A 30 kg child jumps on the edge (r = 2 m). What is the new rotation rate?

### Lesson 08: Angular Momentum

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Angular momentum is why a bicycle stays upright, why gyroscopes work, and why the Earth has seasons."

**Story**: "The Earth's axis is tilted 23.5°. This tilt, combined with angular momentum, gives us seasons. The same principle explains why a spinning top stays upright - angular momentum resists changes to the rotation axis."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define angular momentum
- Apply conservation of angular momentum
- Solve problems involving rotating systems
- Understand precession and gyroscopic effects

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Spacecraft**: Attitude control
- **Navigation**: Gyroscopes, compasses
- **Sports**: Diving, gymnastics, figure skating
- **Physics**: Atomic structure, quantum mechanics

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the angular momentum of a 2 kg sphere (r = 0.5 m) rotating at 10 rev/s.
2. A spinning top with I = 0.01 kg·m² spins at 100 rev/s. If friction reduces I to 0.008 kg·m², what is the new spin rate?
3. A person on a rotating platform (I = 4 kg·m²) spins at 1 rev/s. If they extend their arms and I becomes 6 kg·m², what happens to the spin rate?
4. Why does a bicycle stay upright when moving?
5. Explain how a gyroscope works in an aircraft navigation system.

### Lesson 09: Oscillations and Waves

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Sound, light, earthquakes, music - all are waves. Understanding waves helps us design better speakers, build earthquake-resistant buildings, and even see inside the human body."

**Story**: "In 1816, René Laennec invented the stethoscope because he was uncomfortable placing his ear against a woman's chest. He rolled up a piece of paper and discovered it amplified heart sounds. He had intuitively understood wave physics - and revolutionized medicine."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define simple harmonic motion
- Analyze springs and pendulums
- Understand wave properties
- Apply wave equations

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Ultrasound, MRI, hearing aids
- **Music**: Sound quality, instrument design
- **Engineering**: Earthquake-resistant buildings
- **Technology**: Fiber optics, wireless communication

**Practice Problems:**
1. A spring stretches 10 cm when a 1 kg mass is attached. What is the spring constant?
2. A mass on a spring oscillates with period 2 s. If the mass is doubled, what is the new period?
3. A pendulum has length 1 m. What is its period?
4. A wave has frequency 440 Hz (concert A) and speed 340 m/s. What is its wavelength?
5. Two speakers are 2 m apart. At what angles will constructive interference occur for 500 Hz sound?

### Lesson 10: Gravitation

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces, but it shapes the universe. It makes planets orbit stars, stars form galaxies, and galaxies cluster together."

**Story**: "In 1687, Newton published the Principia, which included the law of universal gravitation. He showed that the same force that makes an apple fall also keeps the Moon in orbit. This was one of the greatest unifications in science - connecting earthly and celestial mechanics."

**Learning Objectives:**
- State Newton's law of universal gravitation
- Calculate gravitational force between objects
- Understand gravitational potential energy
- Apply Kepler's laws of planetary motion

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Space Exploration**: Orbital mechanics, satellite deployment
- **Astronomy**: Galaxy dynamics, black holes
- **GPS**: Gravitational time dilation corrections
- **Geology**: Tides, ocean currents

**Practice Problems:**
1. What is the gravitational force between Earth (6 × 10²⁴ kg) and the Moon (7.3 × 10²² kg) at 3.8 × 10⁸ m?
2. Calculate the gravitational field strength at Earth's surface.
3. What is the escape velocity from Earth?
4. Calculate the orbital period of the ISS at 400 km altitude.
5. Use Kepler's third law to find the orbital period of Mars (distance from Sun = 2.28 × 10¹¹ m).
