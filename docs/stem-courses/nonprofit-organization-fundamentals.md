## Course Description

**Fundamentos de Organizações Não-Governamentais** (Nonprofit Organization Fundamentals) is an intermediate course that teaches how Brazilian civil-society organizations are *defined, compared, structured, governed, funded, operated and proven accountable*. It opens at the boundary that defines the whole discipline — the **non-distribution constraint**: an NGO is not separated from a company by the absence of revenue, but by the destination of the surplus — and then builds the full machinery of the Brazilian third sector in five arcs.

The **fundamentals arc** (Lessons 01–03) establishes what an NGO is (the contested definitions of the UN, the Council of Europe, the Civil Code, the MROSC and OSCIP; the seven essential criteria; the ICNPO typology), how it differs from for-profit and government organizations across a 15-dimension matrix, and how it survives economically (15 revenue models, resource-dependency theory, the fundraising funnel, CAC/LTV, operating reserves). Lessons 01 and 02 now anchor those definitions in a **six-regime international comparison** (United States, United Kingdom, Germany, France, India and the global indices), so the student sees that Brazil's four-layer architecture is one answer among several to the same question.

The **structure-and-management arc** (Lessons 04–07) covers the four-layer Brazilian legal architecture, fiduciary board governance under the IBGC Code, the impact-promise marketing operation (five-stage funnel, six donation personas, three-hour crisis protocol), and operations — the ten-step program/partnership cycle, MROSC compliance, risk, the 13-KPI panel and the four pillars of sustainability. Lesson 04 places Brazilian legal forms side by side with their foreign counterparts; Lesson 05 adds the 2025–2026 case law on councillor liability.

The **models-and-context arc** (Lessons 08–09) tests social-business readiness against tax risk and reads the Brazilian sector quantitatively: **917,727 OSCs (Ipea, fev/2025)**, an ISP of **R$ 5.8 billion (Censo GIFE 2024–2025)**, R$ 24.3 billion in individual donations (Doação Brasil 2024), and the rule that no figure circulates without source and year. Lesson 09 carries a dedicated **regulatory panorama 2025–2026** plus a five-case reading of the Brazilian sector.

The **institutional-routine arc** (Lessons 10–12) turns to accounting under **ITG 2002 (R1)** with the five mandatory statements and the statutory compliance calendar, people management across CLT, statutory and volunteer regimes, and the two tax worlds — immunities and donor incentives on one side, obligations and the **LC nº 214/2025** reform transition on the other. Lessons 10 and 12 carry explicit **2026 update sections** (the CBS/IBS test year, IN RFB nº 2.307/2026, Decreto nº 12.955/2026) and the TCU, STJ and TST rulings that now delimit what a board may and may not do.

The **relationship-and-value arc** (Lessons 13–15) runs the MROSC partnership cycle end to end and then proves the mission: the results chain, Theory of Change, IRIS+/GRI/IMP/SROI, and finally the integration of **ethics × compliance × impact** (the ECI model) plus a graded capstone that builds a complete Brazilian NGO in six stages over six to eight weeks. Lesson 13 adds the 2026 MROSC execution rules; Lesson 14 grounds measurement in verified advocacy and funding cases; Lesson 15 closes with *what is new in 2026*.

Two design principles run through all fifteen lessons. The first is the distinction between **conformity and change**: a receipt proves that money was spent as planned; only an impact study with a design that permits causal attribution proves that lives changed because of the intervention. The second was added by the enhancement round: *no number without source and year, and no claim without a named organization behind it* — which is why every lesson now carries audited real cases instead of invented ones.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:

1. Define an NGO without a legal category to hide behind — using the five formal definitions (UN ECOSOC 1996/31, Council of Europe ETS 124, Civil Code arts. 53/61/69, MROSC art. 2º I, Lei nº 9.790/1999), the seven essential criteria, and the three-layer institutional/technical/operational framework with its six-question decision checklist.
2. Distinguish what *descaracterizes* an entity (non-distribution failure, non-public purpose) from what merely triggers a sanction, and separate "non-profit" from "no economic activity".
3. Compare the three Brazilian sectors across a 15-dimension matrix and the capital → surplus destination → control axis, including the four labour regimes, the three public-contracting tracks (MROSC, Lei nº 14.133/2021, Lei nº 13.303/2016) and nine hybrid forms.
4. Explain why OSCs exist using Weisbrod, Hansmann, Salamon, Andreoni and Buchanan, and read the 15 revenue models against concentration (HHI), restricted versus unrestricted funds and the NORI reserve standard.
5. Calculate fundraising efficiency — cost per real raised, program ratio, LTV/CAC, retention — using sourced benchmarks (ABCR/Criando 11.8%, Charity Navigator 76% program-ratio score, GIFE 2024–2025).
6. Choose the correct Brazilian legal structure (association vs. foundation, plus OS, OSCIP and CEBAS qualifications) and draft a "stacked" bylaw that survives the Civil Code, OSCIP and MROSC simultaneously.
7. Apply fiduciary duties (loyalty, care, obedience) to board composition, RACI allocation, meeting cadence, conflict-of-interest procedure, remuneration rules, evaluation and succession under the IBGC Code items 3.7, 3.10, 3.11, 3.13, 3.14 and 3.17.
8. Build a donor operation — value proposition, six personas, five-stage funnel with metrics, channel mix, 90-day content plan, brand ethics (no poverty porn, no earmarking promises) and a three-hour crisis protocol.
9. Run the ten-step program/partnership cycle under the MROSC: work plan (art. 22), expenses and indirect costs (art. 46), procurement (art. 34 VIII), amendments (art. 57), monitoring (arts. 58–59) and reporting (arts. 64–72), with a 5×5 risk matrix and a continuity plan.
10. Evaluate social-enterprise readiness across 12 business models and the Dees spectrum, distinguishing revenue drift from mission drift and detecting when a sale becomes habitual economic activity.
11. Quantify the Brazilian third sector without mis-citing it — Ipea, RAIS/MTE and IBGE/Cempre cuts, GIFE ISP, Doação Brasil, volunteering statistics — and never add bases that measure different things.
12. Produce OSC financial statements under **ITG 2002 (R1)** (items 4–27), schedule the compliance calendar (ECD, ECF, DCTFWeb, eSocial, EFD-Reinf) and price the **LC nº 214/2025 art. 9º, §4º** purchase trap into the budget.
13. Classify every person as CLT, statutory or volunteer — drafting a Lei nº 9.608/1998 adhesion term, running eSocial Group 3, choosing the LGPD legal basis and complying with CIPA+A / Lei nº 14.457/2022 and SESMT duties proportionate to size.
14. Explain tax immunity and its conditions (CTN art. 14; Lei nº 9.532/1997 art. 12), CEBAS certification and gratuity, donor deduction ceilings, and the reform timeline (LC 214/2025, LC 224/2025, LC 235/2026) as they apply to OSCs.
15. Select the correct MROSC instrument (term of collaboration, term of fostering, cooperation agreement), run the eight-step partnership cycle with the 90/150-day reporting rule, and map stakeholders by power × interest in four currencies of value.
16. Build a results chain, Theory of Change, indicator matrix and KPI dashboard; distinguish baseline × target and contribution × attribution; and interpret SROI studies (FLUPP, Amigos do Bem, Instituto Baccarelli) and Brazilian practice-asymmetry data.
17. Locate a Brazilian NGO inside the international map — US 501(c)(3) and Form 990, UK Charities Act 2011 and public benefit, German §§ 51–68 AO, French loi du 1er juillet 1901, Indian FCRA 2010 — and read a foreign comparison without importing a foreign rule into Brazilian law.
18. Integrate ethics, compliance and impact in a single decision model (the ECI test) and execute the six-stage capstone — from a 30-word mission to a public impact dashboard — against the published 6-criterion rubric.

## Real-World Examples

### Definition, Scale and Measurement

**Example 1: 917,727 OSCs — and why bases must never be added (Lesson 09).**
Ipea's *Perfil das Organizações da Sociedade Civil no Brasil (2016–2025)* maps **917,727 OSCs (fev/2025, published 01/nov/2025)**. The same institute's portal has also displayed 972 thousand / 644 thousand active (set/2025) and 672,215 active (jan/2026). Against that, RAIS/MTE reports 3.7 million formal links (2023) and IBGE/Cempre 11.3 million establishments (base 2023). These measure different things and are **not additive** — the lesson's golden rule is "number + source + year", and citing a bare number is the most common error in exams, tenders and board reports.

**Example 2: 596,259 FASFIL versus 972 thousand OSCs (Lessons 01, 09).**
The IBGE FASFIL (year-base 2023, released 18/12/2025) counts 596,259 non-profit foundations and associations and 2,684,780 occupants; the Ipea Map (set/2025) counts 972 thousand OSCs, of which 644 thousand are active. Religion alone accounts for 210,696 FASFIL (35.3%). The two series must never be summed or compared without stating the counting rule and the treatment of "inapt"/"suspended" registrations.

**Example 3: "ONG" is not a legal nature (Lessons 01, 08).**
There is no "ONG" in the registry office, in the CNPJ or at the Federal Revenue — only association, foundation, simple society or cooperative. Likewise "social enterprise" is an analytical category (EMES, European Commission) and, in Brazil, a public-policy definition (Decree nº 11.646/2023), not a corporate form. Six of the seven Brazilian organizations in digest 16 are private associations and **none is an OS** — the popular belief that "OS = third sector" does not survive a CNPJ lookup.

### Funding and Economics

**Example 4: GIFE's R$ 5.8 billion ISP (Lessons 02, 03, 06, 08, 09).**
The *Censo GIFE 2024–2025* (12th edition, published 08/12/2025, 138 responses = 84% of members) records a Private Social Investment of **R$ 5.8 billion in 2024** — the largest in the 13-year series, against a record R$ 6.7 billion in 2020. Corporate investment was R$ 3.2 billion (55%); only **15% used tax incentives**; R$ 1.3 billion (23%) was passed on to OSCs, of which just R$ 223 million (17% of those transfers) as unrestricted institutional support. Verification: 5.8 × 0.55 = 3.19. The same census reports boards that are **66% male and 89% white**.

**Example 5: The Brazilian donation market (Lessons 03, 06, 09, 13).**
*Pesquisa Doação Brasil 2024* (IDIS/Ipsos, fieldwork March–June 2024, published 07/08/2025) totals **R$ 24.3 billion** in individual donations: 86% of donors choose the cause carefully, **83% research before giving (+8 p.p. vs. 2022)**, **49% already stopped donating after a negative news story**, and year-on-year giving consistency fell from **69% (2015) to 49% (2024)**, while monthly giving to the same organisation fell from 44% (2022) to 39% (2024). Only 30% consider NGOs trustworthy, against 41% in 2020.

**Example 6: Fundraising cost as investment, not expense (Lesson 03).**
ABCR/Criando (2022 data) puts the Brazilian cost of raising R$ 1 at **~11.8%**, with tax-incentivised giving at the top of the return curve and events at the bottom. The NORI standard recommends an operating reserve of **3 to 6 months**, held as genuinely free cash — *free reserve is not restricted money*.

**Example 7: Four concentration cases read as risk, not as trophies (Lessons 03, 04, 05, 10, 13, 14, 15).**
Fundação Itaú: **93% of R$ 444.2 million (2024)** from financial income — a patrimonial endowment that buys autonomy but concentrates risk in one asset class. Instituto Ayrton Senna: **62% of R$ 41.738 million (2024)** in brand and image royalties — one intangible contract. Instituto Sou da Paz: **65% of R$ 12,792,835 (2022)** from foreign foundations. BRAC: **donations are only 10.0% of BDT 140.6 billion** (FY 30/06/2024), because microfinance brings 67.5% and social enterprises 20.1%. MSF: **97.9% private revenue, 85.3% from individuals (2024)**. Each figure is a governance question, not a medal.

### Law, Governance and Compliance

**Example 8: Brazil's four-layer legal architecture (Lesson 04).**
Constitution (art. 5º, XVII–XXI freedom of association; art. 150, VI, "b"/"c" immunities; art. 195, §7º social-security immunity) → Civil Code (Lei nº 10.406/2002, arts. 53–61 associations, 62–69 foundations) → qualification titles (OS Lei nº 9.637/1998, OSCIP Lei nº 9.790/1999 with its three-year rule, CEBAS LC nº 187/2021) → the MROSC (Lei nº 13.019/2014). Confusing layers — treating a qualification as a legal form, or demanding of the MROSC what belongs to the Civil Code — is the most frequent practical, tender and exam error.

**Example 9: The MROSC instruments and deadlines (Lessons 07, 13, 14).**
Term of collaboration (art. 16, proposed by the administration) × term of fostering (art. 17, proposed by the OSC) × cooperation agreement (art. 5º), with public call as the rule (arts. 24 and 29, with stated exceptions). The OSC files final accounts within **90 days (art. 69)**; the administration analyses within **150 days (art. 71)**; reallocation between expense items is capped at **25% (art. 56)** while goals change only by addendum/annex (art. 57); sanctions run with prior defence (art. 73). The old 15% indirect-cost ceiling (art. 47, repealed) still circulates in tender annexes.

**Example 10: Fiduciary duty with an IBGC anchor (Lesson 05).**
Loyalty means acting only in the organisation's interest and declaring conflicts; care means preparing, reviewing and deciding with reasonable diligence; obedience means keeping activities inside purpose, law and bylaws. Board size reference: odd, **between five and nine** (IBGC Code, 6th ed., 2023); BoardSource (FAQ of 01/08/2025, citing *Leading with Intent*) supplies the international comparison. Executive remuneration of a statutory director runs through Lei nº 9.532/1997, art. 12, §2º, "a" — a path distinct from councillor remuneration (IBGC item 3.14).

**Example 11: LC nº 214/2025, art. 9º, §4º — the purchase trap (Lessons 10, 12, 15).**
Art. 9º, III keeps OSCs immune and §3º makes the immunity cumulatively dependent on CTN art. 14, but **§4º rules that IBS/CBS on purchases is not immune** — the tax becomes a permanent cost with no credit. At a 12% scenario on R$ 1,200,000 of annual purchases that is **R$ 144,000 per year** entering the cost base. Worked examples in Lessons 10 and 12 price this into budgets, ratio calculations and the 2026 obligation calendar.

**Example 12: Accounting that keeps the immunity alive (Lesson 10).**
Lei nº 6.404/1976 applied by analogy; **ITG 2002 (R1)** (CFC Resolution nº 1.409/2012) replaces Capital with Social Equity and profit with surplus/deficit and requires five statements (item 22) plus minimum notes (item 27), where **tax waived must be disclosed as if the obligation were owed**. Worked cases include a linked grant, a program ratio of 0.78, a 12-month budget, an immune purchase, and an immune OSC with R$ 3,900,000 revenue deciding whether it must file the ECD. NBC TG 51 restructures the DRE into five categories from 01/01/2027.

**Example 13: The volunteer boundary (Lesson 11).**
A "volunteer" working 40 h/week, fixed schedule, under a coordinator's direction, with a monthly allowance is an employment relationship in disguise. Lei nº 9.608/1998 requires a written adhesion term, no employment tie and expense reimbursement only; employer charges on a CLT post run roughly **26.8%–28.8%** on top of gross salary; eSocial Group 3 governs events; LGPD needs a legal basis for health data; CIPA+A (NR-5, Portaria MTP nº 4.219/2022) plus Lei nº 14.457/2022 art. 23 impose annual anti-harassment training and a reporting channel.

### Marketing, Operations and Social Enterprise

**Example 14: The impact promise and the funnel (Lesson 06).**
An NGO sells a promise of transformation the buyer cannot inspect before paying. The five-stage funnel runs reach → interest → conversion → retention → major gift, with CAC, LTV and the LTV/CAC ≥ 3 rule, plus the hidden cost of platform fees. Channel benchmarks (TIC OSCs 2025 for Pix penetration, e-mail and social), Google Ad Grants, and a three-hour crisis protocol complete the operation; corporate cause-marketing moved **R$ 6.2 billion (+19.4%)** in BISC 2025, with 76% of companies funding OSCs.

**Example 15: Output, outcome and the 13-KPI panel (Lessons 07, 14).**
1,000 meals served is an output; 80% of students improving grades is an outcome; a 30% fall in dropout is an impact. The operations panel carries 13 KPIs with thresholds such as ≥85% targets met, ≥4.0 satisfaction, ≥45% donor retention, ≤8.5% fundraising cost, ≤30% source concentration and ≥3 months of reserve.

**Example 16: Selling without drifting (Lesson 08).**
Twelve NGO business models are tested against tax risk: a sale that becomes habitual economic activity loses exemption. Selling to the State has exactly two doors — the MROSC (a partnership, not a sale) or pregão under Lei nº 14.133/2021 (a sale, with arts. 62–70 qualification); **art. 4º of Lei nº 14.133/2021 is a micro/small-enterprise rule** (TCU, Acórdão nº 1425/2026-Plenário), not a partnership shortcut. The B2B cycle averages **10.1 months** (6sense, 2025) and a prior favourite wins 77–80% of deals.

### Impact Evidence

### International Regimes (digest 17)

**Example 21: The United States — exemption with an absolute political ban (Lessons 01, 02, 04).**
Every 501(c)(3) is *absolutely prohibited* from intervening in a campaign for or against a candidate — the Johnson Amendment of 1954 (100 Cong. Rec. 9604), applied in *Branch Ministries v. Rossotti* (D.C. Cir. 2000) and revisited in *NRB v. Bessent* (order of 31/03/2026); *Association of the Bar v. Commissioner*, 858 F.2d 876 (2d Cir. 1988) held that a "non-partisan" rating was already prohibited intervention. Exemption also requires a public interest (Treas. Reg. 1.501(c)(3)-1(d)(1)(ii)); private inurement is barred and excess benefit is punished under IRC §4958. Form 990 is due on the 15th day of the 5th month with a 6-month extension (Form 8868), triggered at US$ 200k revenue or US$ 500k assets. Giving USA 2026 puts US charitable giving at **US$ 617.20 billion in 2025 (+5.7%)** — 64% individuals, 19% foundations, 7% corporations, US$ 62.19 bn bequests — and DAF assets reached **US$ 327.87 billion across 3.59 million accounts (FY2024)** with a 25.2% payout.

**Example 22: The United Kingdom — public benefit is never presumed (Lessons 01, 02, 04, 10).**
Charities Act 2011, ss. 2–4 (with s. 4(2)) requires demonstration of public benefit; registration starts at **£ 5,000** of income. The Charity Commission's data for July–September 2025 records **171,153 charities, £ 104 billion of income and 923,536 trustees**. Accounts follow SORP 2019 for periods beginning up to 31/12/2025 and **SORP 2026 from 01/01/2026** — the British counterpart of the Brazilian ITG 2002 (R1) transition taught in Lesson 10.

**Example 23: Germany, France and India — three different doors (Lessons 01, 02, 04).**
Germany treats *gemeinnützig* as a **tax status under §§ 51–68 AO** with verification of actual management (BFH, V R 20/5, 05/09/2024), not as a corporate form. France's *loi du 1er juillet 1901* guarantees freedom of association (art. 2) but personality arises only on prefectural declaration, published in the JOAFE within one month. India's **FCRA 2010** (amended 2020) forces foreign-funded NGOs into a dedicated SBI branch in New Delhi (§ 17), bans pass-through of foreign funds (§ 7) and requires Aadhaar/passport data of office holders (§ 12A) — the contrast Lesson 04 uses to show that Brazilian qualification titles are not corporate forms either.

**Example 24: Global benchmarks (Lessons 03, 06, 09).**
The CAF World Giving Report 2025 covers **101 countries**: 64% donated money and 26% volunteered (average 9 hours) in 2024. The Global Philanthropy Environment Index 2025 (95 economies, 173 experts, Indiana University) puts cross-border flows at the weakest score, **3.40, down from 3.58 in 2018**. AmeriCorps (2024) counts **75.7 million US volunteers and 4.99 billion hours worth US$ 167.2 billion** (Sep/2022–Sep/2023); Johns Hopkins' Comparative Nonprofit Sector Project remains the reference for sector-size comparisons and GivingTuesday 2025 supplies the campaign-day benchmark.

## Story: Creating and Proving Value

> **Hook**: "An NGO is defined less by what it raises than by where the surplus goes — and proven less by what it spends than by what changes because it spent it."

> **Story**: The course follows a single arc across fifteen lessons. Lessons 01–03 draw the boundary: an NGO is not a company with less money, it is an organization whose surplus can never leave the mission — a boundary the UN, the Council of Europe, the Civil Code and the MROSC each define *for a different purpose*, which is why the honest answer to "is this an NGO?" is always "definition for what?", and why asking the same question in Washington, London, Berlin, Paris and New Delhi gets five different answers. Lessons 04–09 then build the machine: a four-layer legal architecture, a board that governs instead of micromanages, an operation that converts promise into delivery, a social business that sells without drifting, and a sector whose real size (917,727 OSCs, R$ 5.8 billion of ISP, R$ 24.3 billion of individual giving) has to be cited with source and year or it becomes an error. Lessons 10–14 turn to routine and proof — accounting that keeps immunity alive, people classified correctly before anyone signs, two tax worlds kept apart, partnerships measured in 90 and 150 days, and the results chain that separates a receipt from a change. Lesson 15 stitches the threads into one decision model — ethics × compliance × impact — and hands the student a construction project.

> **Connection**: Every concept converges on the closing question of Lesson 15 — *"how do we prove, with integrity, that the entity fulfils its mission with the resources it receives?"* The capstone executes that answer end to end, from a 30-word mission statement to a public impact dashboard, graded by a rubric that deliberately gives 60% to legal correctness, integration and ethics: a visually perfect project that picks the wrong MROSC instrument does not pass, because that is precisely the error that destroys partnerships and immunities in real life. The enhancement round made that test concrete — a project that signs a term of fostering without a disbursement schedule now also fails Acórdão 1355/2025, and one that budgets corporate donations without the 10% linear cut fails IN RFB nº 2.307/2026.

### Lesson 01: O Que é uma Organização Não-Governamental?

## Assessment Rubric

### Final Project (Capstone) — Lesson 15

The capstone runs in **six stages over six to eight weeks**. Stage weights (the *pesos* of the project plan):

| Stage | Main deliverable | Weight | Week |
|-------|------------------|--------|------|
| **1. Concept and legal structure** | Institutional identity (mission ≤ 30 words + public + territory) and an annotated draft bylaw | **20%** | 1 |
| **2. Governance** | Governance Manual: 5–9 member board, term limits with a re-election cap, signed code of ethics, interests declaration, reporting channel | **20%** | 2 |
| **3. Sustainability and fundraising** | Plan of 3–5 sources, balanced budget, 3–6 month reserve, funnel with Pix and recurrence; **no source above 40%** | **20%** | 3 |
| **4. Operations** | Operations Manual: processes, 5×5 risk matrix, AI policy, LGPD plan, volunteering under Lei nº 9.608/1998 | **15%** | 4–5 |
| **5. Impact and compliance** | Theory of Change + 4–6 indicator panel and a Compliance Manual (post-LC nº 214/2025 regime, 30-day transparency, correct MROSC instrument) | **25%** | 6–7 |
| **6. Integrated dossier and defence** | Final dossier + 10-minute oral defence + peer ethical review (ECI test) | **pass/fail requirement** | 7–8 |
| **Total** | 5 weighted deliverables + integrated dossier and defence | **100%** | **6–8** |

Stages 1–5 are graded; stage 6 is a **requirement**: without the integrated dossier and the defence the final grade is zero, regardless of partial scores (20 + 20 + 20 + 15 + 25 = 100%).

Evaluation rubric (6 criteria, published in the lesson and used for grading):

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent (90–100) | Sufficient (70–89) | Needs improvement (< 70) |
|-----------|--------|--------------------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| **Completeness of deliverables** | **15%** | All 5 deliverables plus dossier and defence complete, no omissions | 5 deliverables present, 1 with minor gaps | 3 or more deliverables missing or incomplete |
| **Legal and tax correctness** | **20%** | Legal form, MROSC instruments and tax regime correct and justified | Pointed articulation errors with no practical consequence | Structural errors (e.g. "convênio" with an OSC, OSCIP in year 1, distribution of surpluses) |
| **Integration of the 14 prior lessons** | **20%** | Connects concepts from at least 8 lessons fluently with coherent examples | Connects concepts from 5 to 7 lessons | Fragmented connections, 4 lessons or fewer |
| **Viability and action** | **15%** | Plan with deadlines, owners, resources and verifiable metrics | General plan with some specific actions | Vague plan without deadlines or owners |
| **Ethics and transparency** | **20%** | Interests declaration, reporting channel, 30-day rule, AI policy and data limitations | Covers most ethical items | Omits transparency, conflict of interest, AI policy or limitations |
| **Clarity and organization** | **10%** | Logical structure, well-used tables and diagrams, correct text | Clear organization with pointed flaws | Disorganized, with errors that hinder reading |
| **Total** | **100%** | — | — | — |

**Final grade:** ≥ 90 = **Excellent** · 70–89 = **Sufficient** · < 70 = **Failed (redo the flagged stages)**.

The rubric is deliberately asymmetric: **legal correctness + integration + ethics total 60%**. A visually impeccable project that selects the wrong MROSC instrument, budgets corporate donations without the 10% linear cut, or hides a data limitation does not pass, because that is precisely the error that destroys partnerships, immunities and reputation in real life. Two worked capstones are graded as references: **Instituto Ponte Viva** (education) and **Instituto Raízes da Serra** (culture and environment).
