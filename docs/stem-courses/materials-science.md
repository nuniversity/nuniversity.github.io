## Course Description

Structure, properties, processing, and performance of engineering materials. This interdisciplinary course connects **chemistry, physics, and engineering** to understand why materials behave the way they do. Students will learn through **interactive simulations, real-world case studies, and design challenges**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Relate atomic structure to material properties
2. Understand crystalline structures and defects
3. Analyze mechanical properties (stress, strain, hardness)
4. Explain phase diagrams and transformations
5. Select appropriate materials for engineering applications

## Real-World Examples

### Aerospace Engineering

**Example 1: Jet Engine Turbine Blades**
Turbine blades operate at 1,700°C while spinning at 10,000 RPM. Single-crystal nickel superalloys with thermal barrier coatings make this possible. Understanding materials science is essential for designing these components.

**Example 2: Carbon Fiber Composites**
Boeing 787 Dreamliner is 50% composite by weight. Carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) is stronger than steel but lighter than aluminum, reducing fuel consumption by 20%.

### Biomedical Engineering

**Example 3: Hip Implants**
Hip replacements must be biocompatible, strong, and wear-resistant. Titanium alloys with hydroxyapatite coatings bond to bone and last 20+ years.

**Example 4: Shape-Memory Alloys**
Nitinol (nickel-titanium) remembers its shape. Used in stents that expand inside arteries and dental braces that apply constant pressure.

### Civil Engineering

**Example 5: High-Strength Concrete**
Modern concrete can withstand 100 MPa or more. Understanding the chemistry of cement hydration and aggregate selection creates durable structures.

**Example 6: Seismic Isolation Bearings**
Lead-rubber bearings absorb earthquake energy, protecting buildings. Materials science creates bearings that are stiff enough for daily use but flexible during earthquakes.

### Mechanical Engineering

**Example 7: Cutting Tools**
Tungsten carbide tools cut steel at high speeds. Understanding hardness, wear resistance, and thermal conductivity helps select the right tool material.

**Example 8: Spring Design**
Springs store elastic energy. The choice of steel alloy, heat treatment, and wire diameter determines spring constant and fatigue life.

### Electronics

**Example 9: Semiconductor Materials**
Silicon dominates electronics, but gallium nitride (GaN) is revolutionizing power electronics. Understanding band gaps and doping is essential for semiconductor engineering.

**Example 10: Battery Materials**
Lithium-ion batteries use different materials for cathodes, anodes, and electrolytes. Materials science improves energy density, charging speed, and lifespan.

## Story: The Bridge That Fell

> **Hook**: "In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed not because of poor design, but because engineers didn't understand aerodynamic flutter - a materials and structural dynamics problem."

> **Story**: The bridge was the third-longest suspension bridge in the world. But its narrow, shallow deck acted like an airfoil, catching the wind and oscillating until it tore itself apart. The disaster led to the creation of wind engineering as a discipline.

> **Connection**: Materials science isn't just about properties - it's about understanding how materials behave in real-world conditions. A material that's strong in a lab might fail in the field.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Introduction to Materials Science

**Duration:** 50 minutes

**Hook**: "Every object around you is made of materials. Understanding why materials behave the way they do is the key to engineering."

**Story**: "Materials science is one of the oldest engineering disciplines. The Bronze Age, Iron Age, and Silicon Age are all named after materials that changed civilization."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define materials science and engineering
- Understand the materials tetrahedron
- Classify materials (metals, ceramics, polymers, composites)
- Relate structure to properties

**Content Outline:**

#### 1. What is Materials Science? (10 min)
- Structure-property-processing-performance relationship
- The materials tetrahedron
- Historical perspective

#### 2. Classification of Materials (12 min)
- Metals and alloys
- Ceramics and glasses
- Polymers (plastics)
- Composites

#### 3. Atomic Bonding (10 min)
- Ionic bonding
- Covalent bonding
- Metallic bonding
- Van der Waals forces

#### 4. Interactive Elements (8 min)
- **Material Explorer**: Compare properties of different materials
- **Bonding Visualizer**: See how atoms bond
- **Property Chart**: Interactive comparison table

**Real-World Application**: Selecting materials for a bicycle frame

**Practice Problems:**
1. Classify each material: steel, glass, nylon, concrete, carbon fiber.
2. Why are metals good conductors of electricity?
3. What makes ceramics hard but brittle?
4. Explain the materials tetrahedron.
5. Select materials for: a) cooking pot, b) window, c) car tire.

### Lesson 02: Atomic Structure and Bonding

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook": "The properties of materials come from how atoms are arranged and bonded."

**Story": "In 1913, William Henry Bragg and his son William Lawrence Bragg developed X-ray crystallography, allowing us to see atomic arrangements for the first time. They shared the Nobel Prize for this breakthrough."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand atomic structure
- Describe types of chemical bonding
- Relate bonding to material properties
- Calculate density from atomic data

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Semiconductor Engineering**: Band gap engineering
- **Catalyst Design**: Surface chemistry
- **Polymer Science**: Molecular design
- **Metallurgy**: Alloy design

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the density of iron (given atomic radius and crystal structure).
2. Why does diamond have higher hardness than graphite?
3. Explain why copper is a better conductor than silicon.
4. Calculate the number of atoms per unit cell in BCC iron.
5. Why do polymers have low density?

### Lesson 03: Crystal Structures

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Most engineering materials are crystalline - their atoms are arranged in repeating patterns."

**Story**: "In 1985, Robert Curl, Harold Kroto, and Richard Smalley discovered C60 (buckminsterfullerene) - a new form of carbon shaped like a soccer ball. This discovery opened up nanotechnology."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe common crystal structures (BCC, FCC, HCP)
- Understand unit cells and packing
- Calculate density and packing fraction
- Identify crystallographic directions and planes

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Metallurgy**: Understanding grain structure
- **Semiconductor Manufacturing**: Crystal growth
- **Nanotechnology**: Nanoparticle design
- **Geology**: Mineral identification

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the atomic packing factor for FCC.
2. Determine the number of atoms in a BCC unit cell.
3. Find the planar density of (110) plane in BCC iron.
4. Explain why FCC metals are more ductile than BCC metals.
5. Calculate the radius of an iron atom given its density and crystal structure.

---

### Lesson 04: Crystal Defects

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Perfect crystals don't exist in nature. Defects make materials stronger, weaker, or enable new properties."

**Story**: "In 1934, Egon Orowan, Michael Polanyi, and G.I. Taylor independently explained how metals deform by dislocation motion. This understanding revolutionized metallurgy."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Classify crystal defects (point, line, planar, volume)
- Understand how defects affect properties
- Explain work hardening and recovery
- Relate defects to material processing

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Metallurgy**: Strengthening mechanisms
- **Semiconductor Manufacturing**: Defect control
- **Ceramics**: Improving toughness
- **Polymer Processing**: Molecular alignment

**Practice Problems:**
1. What is a vacancy? How does it affect diffusion?
2. Explain how dislocations enable plastic deformation.
3. What is work hardening? How can it be reversed?
4. How do grain boundaries affect strength?
5. Why are single crystals used in turbine blades?

### Lesson 05: Mechanical Properties - Stress and Strain

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "When you stretch a rubber band, it deforms. When you stretch a steel rod, it resists. Understanding this behavior is key to designing safe structures."

**Story**: "In 1676, Robert Hooke discovered that springs stretch proportionally to force - Hooke's Law. This simple relationship is the foundation of structural engineering."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define stress and strain
- Calculate engineering stress and strain
- Understand elastic and plastic deformation
- Draw and interpret stress-strain curves

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Structural Engineering**: Designing beams, columns
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Machine design
- **Civil Engineering**: Foundation design
- **Aerospace**: Aircraft structures

**Practice Problems:**
1. A 10 kN force is applied to a 10 mm diameter rod. Calculate the stress.
2. A 2 m aluminum bar stretches 0.5 mm under load. Calculate the strain.
3. Draw a stress-strain curve for ductile and brittle materials.
4. What is the yield strength? How is it determined?
5. Calculate the elastic modulus of a material that stretches 0.1% under 50 MPa stress.

### Lesson 06: Mechanical Properties - Hardness and Toughness

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Hardness and toughness are different properties. A diamond is hard but not tough - it shatters easily. A rubber mallet is tough but not hard."

**Story": "In 1909, Hugh Sharp developed the Brinell hardness test. It's still used today to test metals. The test is simple: press a steel ball into the material and measure the indentation."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define and measure hardness
- Understand toughness and impact testing
- Compare material properties
- Select materials based on properties

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Tool Design**: Selecting cutting tools
- **Wear Resistance**: Bearing surfaces
- **Impact Engineering**: Crash protection
- **Quality Control**: Material testing

**Practice Problems:**
1. Explain the difference between hardness and toughness.
2. What is the Rockwell hardness scale?
3. Why is hardness important for cutting tools?
4. How does temperature affect toughness?
5. Select materials for: a) hammer head, b) car bumper, c) knife blade.

### Lesson 07: Phase Diagrams

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Phase diagrams are maps that tell you what phase a material is in at any temperature and composition."

**Story**: "In 1906, Hugo Gibbs and Hendrik Roozeboom created the first phase diagrams for steel. These diagrams revolutionized steel production and are still used today."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Read and interpret binary phase diagrams
- Understand phase transformations
- Apply the lever rule
- Relate phase diagrams to material processing

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Steel Production**: Heat treatment design
- **Alloy Design**: Creating new materials
- **Casting**: Solidification control
- **Welding**: Joint design

**Practice Problems:**
1. Read a phase diagram and identify phases present.
2. Apply the lever rule to find phase fractions.
3. What is the eutectic composition?
4. Explain the difference between solid solution and intermetallic compound.
5. Design a heat treatment for a 0.4% carbon steel.

### Lesson 08: Diffusion

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Atoms move through solids - slowly, but continuously. Understanding diffusion is essential for heat treatment, doping semiconductors, and more."

**Story": "In 1855, Adolf Fick developed the equations for diffusion. His laws are still used today to predict how atoms move through materials during heat treatment."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand atomic diffusion mechanisms
- Apply Fick's laws
- Calculate diffusion rates
- Relate diffusion to material processing

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Steel Production**: Carburizing, nitriding
- **Semiconductor Manufacturing**: Doping
- **Ceramics**: Sintering
- **Packaging**: Barrier coatings

**Practice Problems:**
1. What is the difference between vacancy and interstitial diffusion?
2. Calculate the diffusion coefficient given activation energy and temperature.
3. How does grain boundary diffusion differ from bulk diffusion?
4. Explain how carburizing strengthens steel surfaces.
5. Design a diffusion process to dope silicon with phosphorus.

### Lesson 09: Polymers

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Polymers are everywhere - from plastic bags to bulletproof vests. Understanding their molecular structure helps us design better materials."

**Story": "In 1907, Leo Baekeland invented Bakelite - the first fully synthetic plastic. It was used for everything from telephones to jewelry. Today, we produce over 380 million tons of plastic annually."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand polymer structure and classification
- Describe polymerization mechanisms
- Relate molecular weight to properties
- Compare thermoplastics, thermosets, and elastomers

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Packaging**: Food containers, bottles
- **Automotive**: Interior parts, bumpers
- **Medical**: Implants, devices
- **Textiles**: Clothing, ropes

**Practice Problems:**
1. What is the difference between addition and condensation polymerization?
2. How does molecular weight affect polymer properties?
3. Why can't thermosets be recycled?
4. Compare the properties of HDPE and LDPE.
5. Design a polymer for a car bumper (needs impact resistance).

### Lesson 10: Ceramics

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Ceramics are heat-resistant, hard, and chemically stable. They're essential for everything from toilets to space shuttles."

**Story": "The Space Shuttle's thermal protection system used over 24,000 silica tiles. These ceramic tiles could withstand temperatures up to 1,260°C, protecting the shuttle during re-entry."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand ceramic structure and bonding
- Describe ceramic processing methods
- Relate structure to ceramic properties
- Compare ceramics to metals and polymers

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Aerospace**: Thermal protection, engine components
- **Electronics**: Capacitors, insulators
- **Medical**: Dental implants, bone replacements
- **Energy**: Fuel cells, batteries

**Practice Problems:**
1. Why are ceramics hard but brittle?
2. What is sintering? How does it work?
3. Compare the thermal conductivity of metals and ceramics.
4. Why are ceramics used for cutting tools?
5. Design a ceramic for a hip implant.

### Lesson 11: Composites

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Composites combine the best properties of different materials. Wood is nature's composite - strong cellulose fibers in a lignin matrix."

**Story": "In 1966, Rolls-Royce developed the first composite fan blade for a jet engine. Today, composite fan blades are standard in modern jet engines, saving fuel and reducing noise."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand composite classification
- Describe reinforcement and matrix phases
- Calculate composite properties using rule of mixtures
- Compare different composite systems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Aerospace**: Aircraft structures, rocket bodies
- **Automotive**: Body panels, drive shafts
- **Sports**: Tennis rackets, golf clubs, bicycles
- **Civil Engineering**: Bridge decks, strengthening

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the three main types of composites?
2. Calculate the modulus of a unidirectional composite using rule of mixtures.
3. Why are carbon fiber composites so strong?
4. Compare thermoset and thermoplastic matrix composites.
5. Design a composite for a bicycle frame.

### Lesson 12: Materials Selection

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Engineering is about trade-offs. Materials selection is finding the best balance of properties, cost, and manufacturability."

**Story": "In 1993, Mike Ashby developed the Cambridge Materials Selector - a software tool that helps engineers select materials. His method of using property charts revolutionized materials selection."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Apply materials selection methodologies
- Use Ashby charts
- Consider cost, availability, and manufacturability
- Make informed material selection decisions

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Product Design**: Selecting materials for new products
- **Manufacturing**: Choosing production methods
- **Cost Reduction**: Finding cheaper alternatives
- **Sustainability**: Environmental considerations

**Practice Problems:**
1. Use an Ashby chart to select materials for a lightweight beam.
2. Compare materials for a car body (steel, aluminum, CFRP).
3. Select materials for a medical implant (consider biocompatibility).
4. Design a material for a smartphone case (needs to be thin, strong, and lightweight).
5. Evaluate materials for a wind turbine blade.
