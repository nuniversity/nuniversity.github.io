## Course Description

Energy, heat, work, and the laws governing their transformations. This interdisciplinary course connects **mechanical engineering, chemical engineering, physics, and chemistry** to understand energy systems. Students will learn through **real-world case studies, virtual labs, and design challenges**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Apply the first and second laws of thermodynamics
2. Analyze energy systems (power plants, refrigerators, engines)
3. Understand entropy and its implications
4. Perform thermodynamic cycle analysis
5. Design energy-efficient systems

## Real-World Examples

### Power Generation

**Example 1: Coal Power Plant**
A coal power plant converts chemical energy to electrical energy at about 33% efficiency. Understanding thermodynamics helps us improve efficiency and reduce emissions.

**Example 2: Solar Thermal Power**
Concentrated solar power uses mirrors to heat fluids, driving turbines. Thermodynamics helps us optimize the cycle and maximize efficiency.

### Transportation

**Example 3: Car Engine**
A car engine converts fuel to motion at about 25% efficiency. The rest is lost as heat. Understanding thermodynamics helps us design more efficient engines.

**Example 4: Electric Vehicles**
Electric motors are 85-90% efficient, but batteries lose energy during charging. Thermodynamics helps us understand these losses and improve range.

### HVAC

**Example 5: Air Conditioning**
Air conditioners move heat from inside to outside. The coefficient of performance (COP) tells us how efficiently they work. Thermodynamics helps us design better systems.

**Example 6: Heat Pumps**
Heat pumps can provide heating and cooling. They're 3-4 times more efficient than electric heaters because they move heat rather than create it.

### Industrial Processes

**Example 7: Refrigeration**
Refrigerators and freezers preserve food by removing heat. The vapor-compression cycle is the most common refrigeration method.

**Example 8: Chemical Plants**
Chemical plants use thermodynamics to design processes. Energy balance, heat exchangers, and reaction kinetics all depend on thermodynamic principles.

### Environmental

**Example 9: Climate Change**
Understanding energy balance helps us model climate change. Thermodynamics explains how greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere.

**Example 10: Renewable Energy**
Wind, solar, and hydro power all involve energy conversion. Thermodynamics helps us optimize these systems and predict their performance.

## Story: The Carnot Engine

> **Hook**: "In 1824, a 28-year-old French engineer named Sadi Carnot published a small book that would change the world. He showed that no engine can be 100% efficient."

> **Story**: Carnot studied steam engines and asked: what's the maximum efficiency possible? He discovered that efficiency depends only on temperature difference - not the engine design. This simple insight led to the second law of thermodynamics.

> **Connection**: Every engine, from car engines to power plants, is limited by Carnot efficiency. Understanding this limit helps engineers design better energy systems.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Introduction to Thermodynamics

**Duration:** 50 minutes

**Hook**: "Energy is everywhere - in the food you eat, the fuel you burn, the electricity you use. Thermodynamics explains how energy transforms."

**Story**: "The word 'thermodynamics' comes from Greek: 'therme' (heat) and 'dynamis' (power). The science began with the need to improve steam engines during the Industrial Revolution."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define thermodynamics and its applications
- Understand energy, heat, and work
- Learn the zeroth law of thermodynamics
- Apply temperature measurements

**Content Outline:**

#### 1. What is Thermodynamics? (10 min)
- Energy transformations
- System and surroundings
- Types of systems (open, closed, isolated)

#### 2. Energy, Heat, and Work (12 min)
- Forms of energy
- Heat transfer mechanisms
- Work done by/on systems

#### 3. Temperature and Zeroth Law (10 min)
- Temperature scales
- Thermal equilibrium
- Temperature measurement

#### 4. Interactive Elements (8 min)
- **Energy Flow Visualizer**: See energy transformations
- **Temperature Converter**: Convert between scales
- **System Boundary Tool**: Define systems

**Real-World Application**: Designing a solar water heater

**Practice Problems:**
1. Classify each system: open, closed, or isolated.
2. Convert 72°F to Celsius and Kelvin.
3. Explain the zeroth law of thermodynamics.
4. Give 3 examples of energy transformation.
5. What is the difference between heat and temperature?

### Lesson 02: First Law of Thermodynamics

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Energy cannot be created or destroyed - only transformed. This is the first law of thermodynamics."

**Story": "In 1843, James Joule published his experiments showing that heat is a form of energy. He measured the work done by falling weights stirring water, proving that mechanical work converts to heat."

**Learning Objectives:**
- State the first law of thermodynamics
- Apply energy conservation to closed systems
- Calculate internal energy changes
- Understand enthalpy

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Power Generation**: Energy balance
- **Chemical Engineering**: Reaction energy
- **HVAC**: Heating and cooling loads
- **Automotive**: Engine efficiency

**Practice Problems:**
1. A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. What is the change in internal energy?
2. Calculate the enthalpy change for a reaction.
3. Explain why the first law is energy conservation.
4. A piston compresses gas with 100 J of work. If no heat is transferred, what happens to internal energy?
5. Design a simple energy balance for a home heating system.

### Lesson 03: Enthalpy

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Enthalpy is the heat content of a system at constant pressure. It's why some reactions get hot and others get cold."

**Story**: "In 1840, Germain Hess discovered that the total enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the path. This allows us to calculate reactions we can't measure directly."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define enthalpy and its significance
- Calculate enthalpy changes
- Apply Hess's law
- Understand calorimetry

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Chemical Engineering**: Reaction design
- **Fuel Analysis**: Heating values
- **Environmental**: Combustion emissions
- **Food Science**: Calorimetry

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the enthalpy of combustion for methane.
2. Use Hess's law to find the enthalpy of formation.
3. What is the difference between ΔH and ΔU?
4. Design a calorimeter to measure food energy.
5. Calculate the heat released by burning 1 kg of gasoline.

---

### Lesson 04: Second Law of Thermodynamics

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Some processes are impossible. You can't unscramble an egg or cool a room without a refrigerator. The second law explains why."

**Story": "In 1850, Rudolf Clausius formulated the second law: heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot. This simple statement has profound implications for energy systems."

**Learning Objectives:**
- State the second law of thermodynamics
- Understand entropy and its meaning
- Apply the Clausius inequality
- Calculate entropy changes

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Power Generation**: Efficiency limits
- **Refrigeration**: Minimum work requirements
- **Environmental**: Irreversible processes
- **Information Theory**: Entropy and information

**Practice Problems:**
1. Explain why heat flows from hot to cold.
2. Calculate the entropy change when 1 kg of ice melts.
3. Why can't a refrigerator cool a room without electricity?
4. What is the maximum efficiency of a heat engine between 500 K and 300 K?
5. Explain entropy using a deck of cards analogy.

### Lesson 05: Entropy

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Entropy is a measure of disorder. It always increases in natural processes - that's why you can't unscramble an egg."

**Story": "Ludwig Boltzmann showed that entropy is related to the number of ways molecules can be arranged. His famous equation S = k ln W connects microscopic disorder to macroscopic properties."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand entropy at molecular level
- Calculate entropy changes
- Apply entropy to real-world problems
- Understand the arrow of time

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Information Theory**: Data compression
- **Biology**: Life and entropy
- **Cosmology**: Heat death of universe
- **Engineering**: Maximum efficiency

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the entropy change when 2 mol of ideal gas expands from 10 L to 20 L.
2. Why does a hot cup of coffee cool down?
3. Explain the relationship between entropy and probability.
4. What is the entropy change of the universe for a reversible process?
5. How does photosynthesis relate to entropy?

### Lesson 06: Third Law of Thermodynamics

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook**: "At absolute zero, entropy reaches its minimum. But can we actually reach absolute zero?"

**Story": "In 1907, Walther Nernst formulated the third law: it's impossible to reach absolute zero. This is why we can't cool things to 0 K, no matter how hard we try."

**Learning Objectives:**
- State the third law of thermodynamics
- Understand absolute zero
- Calculate entropy at different temperatures
- Apply the third law to real problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Cryogenics**: Superconductors, medical imaging
- **Space Exploration**: Deep space cooling
- **Quantum Computing**: Operating temperatures
- **Materials Science**: Low-temperature properties

**Practice Problems:**
1. Why can't we reach absolute zero?
2. What is the entropy of a perfect crystal at 0 K?
3. Explain the third law using a chessboard analogy.
4. Why do some materials become superconducting at low temperatures?
5. Design a system to cool something to very low temperatures.

### Lesson 07: Ideal Gases

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Ideal gases follow simple rules. Real gases are more complex, but ideal gas behavior is a good approximation at normal conditions."

**Story": "In 1834, Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron combined Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's laws into one equation: PV = nRT. This equation describes ideal gas behavior."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Apply the ideal gas law
- Understand gas processes (isothermal, isobaric, isochoric)
- Calculate work done by gases
- Relate gas behavior to thermodynamics

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Engine Design**: Combustion modeling
- **HVAC**: Air conditioning systems
- **Chemical Engineering**: Gas reactions
- **Environmental**: Atmospheric science

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the volume of 2 mol of ideal gas at 300 K and 1 atm.
2. What is the work done when a gas expands from 10 L to 20 L at constant pressure?
3. Compare isothermal and adiabatic expansion.
4. Why does a balloon pop when heated?
5. Design a gas storage system for a given pressure and temperature.

### Lesson 08: Vapor Power Cycles

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Most electricity comes from steam turbines. Understanding the Rankine cycle helps us design more efficient power plants."

**Story": "In 1859, William Rankine published his analysis of the steam cycle. His work is still the basis for modern power plant design."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand the Rankine cycle
- Calculate cycle efficiency
- Improve cycles with reheating and regeneration
- Compare different cycle configurations

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Power Generation**: Coal, nuclear, solar thermal plants
- **Combined Cycles**: Gas + steam turbines
- **Cogeneration**: Power and heat
- **Renewable Energy**: Geothermal plants

**Practice Problems:**
1. Draw the T-s diagram for a Rankine cycle.
2. Calculate the efficiency of a simple Rankine cycle.
3. How does reheat improve efficiency?
4. Design a regenerative Rankine cycle.
5. Compare Rankine and Brayton cycles.

### Lesson 09: Refrigeration Cycles

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Refrigerators and air conditioners move heat from cold to hot - but they need work input."

**Story": "In 1834, Jacob Perkins built the first practical refrigerator using the vapor compression cycle. His design is still the basis for modern refrigeration."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand the vapor-compression cycle
- Calculate coefficient of performance (COP)
- Analyze absorption refrigeration
- Compare different refrigeration methods

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Food Preservation**: Refrigerators, freezers
- **HVAC**: Air conditioning, heat pumps
- **Industrial**: Cold storage, process cooling
- **Medical**: Vaccine storage, cryogenics

**Practice Problems:**
1. Draw the vapor-compression cycle on a P-h diagram.
2. Calculate the COP of a refrigerator operating between 5°C and 30°C.
3. How does a heat pump differ from a refrigerator?
4. Design a refrigeration system for a cold storage facility.
5. Compare vapor-compression and absorption cycles.

- Understand the Otto and Diesel cycles

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Automotive**: Car engines
- **Aviation**: Jet engines
- **Power Generation**: Gas turbines
- **Hybrid Systems**: Combined cycles

**Practice Problems:**
1. Draw the P-V and T-s diagrams for the Otto cycle.
2. Calculate the efficiency of an Otto cycle with compression ratio 8.
3. Compare the Otto and Diesel cycles.
4. Design a gas turbine for a given power output.
