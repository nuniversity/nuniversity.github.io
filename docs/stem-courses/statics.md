## Course Description

Equilibrium of particles and rigid bodies, structural analysis, and mechanical systems. This course connects fundamental statics to **bridge design, building construction, cranes, and everyday structures**. Students will learn through **real-world case studies, virtual labs, and design challenges**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Draw and analyze free-body diagrams
2. Apply equilibrium equations
3. Analyze trusses, frames, and machines
4. Calculate moments and couples
5. Understand friction and center of gravity

---

## Real-World Examples

### Civil Engineering

**Example 1: Bridge Design**
The Golden Gate Bridge has been standing since 1937 because engineers understood statics. The cables carry tension, the towers carry compression, and the trusses distribute loads - all principles you'll learn here.

**Example 2: Building Stability**
Every building you enter is a triumph of statics. Forces must balance perfectly - gravity pulling down, the foundation pushing up, wind pushing sideways, and the structure resisting.

### Mechanical Engineering

**Example 3: Cranes**
A crane's counterweight must create enough moment to balance the load. Understanding moments helps engineers design cranes that can lift heavy loads without tipping.

**Example 4: Car Design**
A car's weight distribution affects handling. Engineers use statics to position the engine and fuel tank for optimal balance.

### Everyday Life

**Example 5: Carrying Objects**
When you carry a heavy bag, you instinctively hold it close to your body to reduce the moment arm. You're applying statics principles without knowing it!

**Example 6: Leaning Ladders**
A ladder leans against a wall at a safe angle. Understanding forces and moments helps you avoid dangerous falls.

### Architecture

**Example 7: Roof Design**
The triangular shape of roof trusses makes them incredibly strong. Understanding truss analysis helps architects design safe, efficient roofs.

**Example 8: Earthquake Resistance**
In earthquake-prone areas, buildings need flexible structures that can sway without collapsing. Statics helps engineers design these systems.

## Story: The Tacoma Narrows Bridge

> **Hook**: "In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 40 mph wind. The video of this disaster is still shown to engineering students worldwide."

> **Story**: The bridge was designed without understanding aerodynamic forces. Wind caused the bridge to oscillate until it tore itself apart. This disaster taught engineers that statics must consider dynamic forces too - leading to modern wind-resistant design.

> **Connection**: Every bridge you cross has been designed using the principles you'll learn here. The lessons from past failures make today's structures safer.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Forces Introduction

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook**: "Forces are everywhere - gravity pulling you down, friction keeping you from slipping, tension holding up a bridge."

**Story**: "Archimedes once said, 'Give me a lever long enough and I shall move the world.' This statement captures the essence of statics - understanding forces and moments to design structures that work."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define force and its properties
- Represent forces as vectors
- Calculate resultant forces
- Apply equilibrium conditions

**Content Outline:**

#### 1. What is a Force? (10 min)
- Definition and properties
- Contact forces vs. field forces
- Units (Newtons, pounds)

#### 2. Force Vectors (12 min)
- Representation
- Magnitude and direction
- Resultant force

#### 3. Free-Body Diagrams (15 min)
- Drawing FBDs
- Common forces (gravity, normal, friction, tension)
- Equilibrium conditions

#### 4. Equilibrium Equations (10 min)
- ΣFx = 0
- ΣFy = 0
- Solving problems

#### 5. Interactive Elements (8 min)
- **Force Visualizer**: Drag vectors, see resultant
- **Free-Body Diagram Builder**: Click to add forces
- **Equilibrium Calculator**: Solve for unknowns

**Real-World Application**: Designing a simple bracket to hold a shelf

**Practice Problems:**
1. Two forces of 10 N east and 15 N north act on a point. Find the resultant.
2. A 50 kg box sits on a floor. Draw the free-body diagram.
3. Two forces act on a bracket: 200 N at 30° and 150 N at 120°. Find the resultant.
4. A traffic light hangs from two cables at 45° angles. If the light weighs 100 N, what is the tension in each cable?
5. Design a simple shelf bracket to hold 20 kg.

### Lesson 02: Equilibrium of Particles

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "When you hang a picture on a wall, you want it to stay put. That's equilibrium - all forces perfectly balanced."

**Story**: "The ancient Egyptians built the pyramids using principles of equilibrium. They understood that stones must be carefully balanced to prevent collapse. Their structures have lasted over 4,000 years."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Apply equilibrium conditions to particles
- Solve 2D and 3D equilibrium problems
- Understand concurrent forces
- Use graphical and analytical methods

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Construction**: Supporting structures
- **Transportation**: Cable tensions, anchor points
- **Sports**: Rope climbing, suspension bridges
- **Everyday**: Hanging objects, supporting loads

**Practice Problems:**
1. A 5 kg mass hangs from two cables at 30° and 45° from vertical. Find the tension in each cable.
2. Three forces act on a bracket: 100 N up, 80 N right, and unknown force F. Find F for equilibrium.
3. A sign hangs from two cables. The left cable is at 30°, the right at 60°. If the sign weighs 200 N, find tensions.
4. A weightlifter holds 200 kg overhead. What force must each arm exert?
5. Design a cable system to support a 1000 N sign with cables at 45° angles.

### Lesson 03: Equilibrium of Rigid Bodies

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "When you open a door, you're applying a moment. Understanding moments helps engineers design everything from hinges to cranes."

**Story**: "In 1834, Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine - a mechanical calculator. His designs required precise understanding of moments and forces to ensure the gears would work correctly."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define moment of a force
- Calculate moments about a point
- Apply equilibrium equations to rigid bodies
- Understand couples and their effects

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Lever design, gear systems
- **Civil Engineering**: Beam analysis, column design
- **Everyday**: Opening doors, using tools
- **Sports**: Bat swing, golf club design

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the moment of a 50 N force at 0.3 m from a pivot.
2. A beam has a 100 N load at 2 m from the left end. What reaction is needed at the right end (4 m total)?
3. A 10 N force acts at 3 m from a point. What equal and opposite force at 5 m creates the same moment?
4. A see-saw has a 30 kg child at 1.5 m from the pivot. Where should a 20 kg child sit to balance?
5. Design a lever system to lift a 200 N rock with 50 N of effort.

### Lesson 04: Structural Analysis Introduction

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Every bridge, building, and airplane is a structure. Understanding how they work starts with structural analysis."

**Story**: "The Roman aqueducts have stood for 2,000 years. Roman engineers understood that arches distribute forces efficiently - a principle still used in modern bridges and tunnels."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Classify structures (trusses, frames, machines)
- Determine if a structure is determinate
- Identify zero-force members
- Apply method of joints and sections

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Civil Engineering**: Bridge and building design
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Machine frames
- **Aerospace**: Aircraft structures
- **Architecture**: Structural systems

**Practice Problems:**
1. Classify each structure as truss, frame, or machine.
2. Determine if a 10-bar truss is determinate.
3. Identify zero-force members in a given truss.
4. Using method of joints, find forces in members AB and BC.
5. Using method of sections, find force in member DE.

### Lesson 05: Trusses

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Trusses are everywhere - in bridges, roofs, cranes, and even bicycles. Their triangular shape makes them incredibly strong."

**Story**: "The Forth Bridge in Scotland (1890) was the first major structure made of steel. Its truss design can support 6,000 tons - still in use today after over 130 years."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Analyze simple trusses
- Apply method of joints
- Apply method of sections
- Identify tension and compression members

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Civil Engineering**: Bridge design, roof structures
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Crane booms, machine frames
- **Aerospace**: Aircraft fuselage, wing structures
- **Architecture**: Space frames, canopies

**Practice Problems:**
1. Analyze a simple 5-bar truss using method of joints.
2. Find forces in members using method of sections.
3. Identify which members are in tension vs. compression.
4. Design a truss to span 10 meters with a 5 kN load.
5. Analyze a roof truss with distributed loads.

### Lesson 06: Frames and Machines

**Duration:** 75 minutes

**Hook**: "Frames and machines have moving parts - they're designed to transmit and modify forces."

**Story**: "The trebuchet was the ultimate medieval siege engine. Understanding frames and moments allowed engineers to design machines that could throw 100 kg stones over 300 meters."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Analyze frames and machines
- Understand multi-force members
- Solve for internal forces at pins and connections
- Apply equilibrium to complex structures

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Machine design
- **Civil Engineering**: Building frames
- **Aerospace**: Landing gear, control systems
- **Everyday**: Bicycle frames, furniture

**Practice Problems:**
1. Analyze a simple frame with two members.
2. Find reactions at supports and internal forces.
3. Determine forces at pins connecting members.
4. Design a simple machine to lift a load.
5. Analyze a bicycle frame under rider weight.

### Lesson 07: Friction

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Friction is both friend and foe - it lets us walk but also wears out our shoes."

**Story**: "In ancient times, workers dragged stone blocks on wooden sledges. They poured water on the ground to reduce friction - an early application of tribology, the science of friction."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand static and kinetic friction
- Calculate friction forces
- Apply friction to real-world problems
- Understand angle of repose

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Automotive**: Tire grip, braking systems
- **Construction**: Material handling, safety
- **Sports**: Shoe design, track surfaces
- **Everyday**: Walking, opening jars

**Practice Problems:**
1. A 50 kg box sits on a floor (μs = 0.4, μk = 0.3). What force is needed to start it moving?
2. A car brakes on a wet road (μ = 0.6). If it's traveling at 30 m/s, how far does it slide?
3. Calculate the angle of repose for sand (μs = 0.7).
4. A ladder leans against a wall at 60°. What friction force prevents slipping?
5. Design a brake system for a bicycle.

### Lesson 08: Center of Gravity

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "The center of gravity is the point where all the weight seems to concentrate. It determines whether objects stand or fall."

**Story**: "Tightrope walkers use long poles to lower their center of gravity and increase stability. Understanding center of gravity helps us design safe structures and vehicles."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define center of gravity
- Calculate center of gravity for simple shapes
- Understand stability and tipping
- Apply center of gravity to engineering problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Automotive**: Vehicle stability, rollover prevention
- **Aerospace**: Aircraft balance, rocket design
- **Construction**: Building stability
- **Sports**: Athletic performance

**Practice Problems:**
1. Find the center of gravity of a uniform rod.
2. Calculate the center of gravity of a T-shaped beam.
3. Why is a wider base more stable?
4. At what angle will a box tip over?
5. Design a stable structure with a high center of gravity.

---

### Lesson 09: Moments of Inertia

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Moment of inertia is the rotational equivalent of mass - it measures how hard it is to rotate an object."

**Story**: "Figure skaters spin faster when they pull their arms in because they reduce their moment of inertia. Angular momentum is conserved, so smaller moment means faster spin."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define moment of inertia
- Calculate moment of inertia for simple shapes
- Apply parallel axis theorem
- Use moment of inertia in equilibrium problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Mechanical Engineering**: Flywheel design, rotating machinery
- **Aerospace**: Satellite attitude control
- **Sports**: Equipment design
- **Automotive**: Wheel and tire design

**Practice Problems:**
1. Calculate the moment of inertia of a rod about one end.
2. Find the moment of inertia of a solid cylinder about its axis.
3. Use parallel axis theorem to find moment of inertia of a disk about its edge.
4. Why does a hollow sphere have higher moment of inertia than a solid sphere of the same mass?
5. Design a flywheel to store rotational energy.
