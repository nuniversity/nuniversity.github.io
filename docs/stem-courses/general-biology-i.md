## Course Description

Cell biology, biochemistry, molecular biology, genetics, and evolution. This course connects fundamental biology to **medicine, genetics, agriculture, and biotechnology**. Students will learn through **virtual microscopy, 3D cell models, and real-world case studies**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Describe cell structure and function
2. Explain cellular respiration and photosynthesis
3. Understand cell division and genetics
4. Apply Mendelian genetics principles
5. Describe DNA structure and function

---

## Real-World Examples

### Medicine & Health

**Example 1: Cancer Biology**
Cancer is uncontrolled cell division. Understanding the cell cycle helps us develop targeted therapies that stop cancer cells without harming healthy cells.

**Example 2: Stem Cell Therapy**
Stem cells can become any cell type. Scientists are using them to repair damaged hearts, restore vision, and treat spinal cord injuries.

### Genetics & Agriculture

**Example 3: GMO Crops**
Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) have DNA from other species. Understanding genetics helps us create crops that resist pests, tolerate drought, and provide more nutrition.

**Example 4: Genetic Testing**
DNA testing can predict disease risk, identify paternity, and solve crimes. Understanding genetics is essential for modern forensics.

### Biotechnology

**Example 5: mRNA Vaccines**
COVID-19 mRNA vaccines use lipid nanoparticles to deliver genetic instructions. Understanding cell biology helps us design better vaccines.

**Example 6: CRISPR Gene Editing**
CRISPR allows precise editing of DNA. This technology could cure genetic diseases, create disease-resistant animals, and even resurrect extinct species.

### Everyday Life

**Example 7: Probiotics**
Yogurt contains beneficial bacteria that help your gut. Understanding cell biology helps us maintain a healthy microbiome.

**Example 8: Food Preservation**
Understanding bacterial growth helps us preserve food safely. Refrigeration slows cell division; canning kills bacteria.

## Story: The Cell's Powerhouse

> **Hook**: "Your body contains about 37 trillion cells, each one a tiny factory converting food into energy 24/7."

> **Story**: In 1961, Albert Einstein was asked what he would most like to know. His answer: "I would like to know how the cell works." Even Einstein recognized that cells are among the most complex machines in the universe.

> **Connection**: Every breath you take delivers oxygen to your cells, where mitochondria convert glucose into ATP - the energy currency of life. Understanding this process helps us understand aging, disease, and exercise.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Introduction to Biology

**Duration:** 50 minutes

**Hook**: "Life is the most complex phenomenon in the universe. From bacteria to blue whales, all life shares common principles."

**Story**: "Charles Darwin spent 20 years gathering evidence before publishing 'On the Origin of Species.' He traveled the world, observing finches, tortoises, and coral reefs. His patience and attention to detail changed our understanding of life itself."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define biology and its branches
- Understand the characteristics of life
- Describe the levels of biological organization
- Apply the scientific method

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Understanding disease
- **Agriculture**: Improving crops
- **Conservation**: Protecting ecosystems
- **Biotechnology**: Creating new technologies

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the 7 characteristics of life?
2. Name 5 branches of biology and what they study.
3. Design an experiment to test whether plants need light to grow.
4. What is the difference between a hypothesis and a theory?
5. List the levels of organization from atom to biosphere.

---

### Lesson 02: Chemistry of Life

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook**: "Your body is a chemical machine. It breaks down food, builds proteins, and repairs damage - all through chemical reactions."

**Story**: "In 1953, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey simulated early Earth conditions and produced amino acids - the building blocks of proteins. This experiment showed how life's chemistry could have begun on primitive Earth."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Identify the four macromolecules
- Understand the structure of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids
- Describe the role of water in biology
- Explain enzyme function

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Nutrition**: Understanding food components
- **Medicine**: Enzyme inhibitors as drugs
- **Forensics**: DNA analysis
- **Sports**: Protein supplements, hydration

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the four main types of macromolecules?
2. What makes water such a good solvent?
3. How do enzymes speed up reactions?
4. Compare starch and cellulose - both are carbohydrates, but one is food and one is fiber. Why?
5. What happens to proteins when they are denatured?

---

### Lesson 03: Cell Structure

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "A cell is like a city. It has a power plant (mitochondria), a factory (ribosomes), a library (nucleus), and a transportation system (ER)."

**Story**: "In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at cork under a microscope and saw tiny boxes. He called them 'cells' after the small rooms where monks lived. He had discovered the fundamental unit of life."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Compare and contrast prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
- Describe the structure and function of cell organelles
- Understand the cell membrane structure
- Relate cell structure to function

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Antibiotics target bacterial cell structures
- **Forensics**: Cell evidence at crime scenes
- **Agriculture**: Genetic modification of cells
- **Technology**: Bioinspired materials

**Practice Problems:**
1. What is the difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?
2. Name 5 organelles and their functions.
3. Why do plant cells have chloroplasts but animal cells don't?
4. What is the role of the cell membrane?
5. How does the structure of mitochondria relate to its function?

---

### Lesson 04: Cell Membrane

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "The cell membrane is a gatekeeper. It decides what enters and leaves, maintaining the delicate balance needed for life."

**Story**: "In 1972, Singer and Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model of the cell membrane. They showed that membranes are not rigid walls but dynamic, fluid structures with proteins floating like icebergs in a sea of lipids."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe the fluid mosaic model
- Understand membrane transport mechanisms
- Explain osmosis and diffusion
- Apply membrane transport to real-world problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Drug delivery systems
- **Agriculture**: Fertilizer absorption
- **Technology**: Artificial membranes
- **Environmental**: Water purification

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the main components of a cell membrane?
2. Diffusion vs. osmosis - what's the difference?
3. What is active transport? Give an example.
4. Why do red blood cells burst in pure water?
5. How do drugs cross the cell membrane?

---

### Lesson 05: Cellular Respiration

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Every time you eat, your cells break down food to make energy. This process is cellular respiration - the engine of life."

**Story**: "In 1937, Hans Krebs discovered the citric acid cycle - a series of chemical reactions that extract energy from food. This discovery earned him the Nobel Prize and gave us the Krebs cycle, named after him."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe the steps of cellular respiration
- Calculate ATP yield from glucose
- Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration
- Explain the role of mitochondria

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Metabolic diseases, diabetes
- **Exercise**: How muscles use energy
- **Agriculture**: Crop yield optimization
- **Biotechnology**: Biofuels

**Practice Problems:**
1. Write the equation for cellular respiration.
2. How many ATP molecules are produced from one glucose molecule?
3. What is the difference between aerobic and anaerobic respiration?
4. Why do muscles burn during intense exercise?
5. How does the mitochondrion's structure relate to its function?

---

### Lesson 06: Photosynthesis

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Photosynthesis is the opposite of respiration. It takes carbon dioxide and water, and with sunlight, makes glucose and oxygen."

**Story": "In 1779, Jan Ingenhousz discovered that plants produce oxygen only in sunlight. He spent three years conducting experiments in his garden, showing that light is essential for photosynthesis."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe the light-dependent and light-independent reactions
- Explain the role of chloroplasts
- Understand the Calvin cycle
- Relate photosynthesis to global carbon cycle

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Agriculture**: Optimizing crop yields
- **Environmental**: Climate change, carbon sequestration
- **Energy**: Artificial photosynthesis
- **Biology**: Food chains, ecosystem energy flow

**Practice Problems:**
1. Write the equation for photosynthesis.
2. What is the role of chlorophyll?
3. Where does the light-dependent reaction occur?
4. What is the Calvin cycle?
5. How does deforestation affect the carbon cycle?

---

### Lesson 07: Cell Division

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Cell division is how we grow, heal, and reproduce. It's the engine of life - and when it goes wrong, diseases like cancer can result."

**Story**: "In 1911, Theodor Boveri proposed that cancer results from chromosome abnormalities. It took nearly a century to prove him right, but his insight launched modern cancer research."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe the cell cycle
- Compare mitosis and meiosis
- Understand the phases of mitosis
- Explain the importance of cell division

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Cancer treatment, regenerative medicine
- **Agriculture**: Plant propagation, hybridization
- **Forensics**: DNA analysis from cell division
- **Biotechnology**: Cloning, stem cell therapy

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the phases of mitosis in order?
2. How does mitosis differ from meiosis?
3. Why is mitosis important for growth and repair?
4. What happens during crossing over in meiosis?
5. How do errors in cell division lead to cancer?

---

### Lesson 08: Mendelian Genetics

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Why do children resemble their parents? Why do some traits skip generations? Mendel's laws explain the patterns of inheritance."

**Story**: "Gregor Mendel was a monk who spent eight years crossing pea plants in his monastery garden. He discovered the laws of inheritance but was ignored for 35 years. When rediscovered in 1900, his work became the foundation of modern genetics."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand Mendel's laws of inheritance
- Use Punnett squares to predict offspring
- Distinguish dominant and recessive traits
- Apply probability to genetics

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Genetic diseases, genetic counseling
- **Agriculture**: Selective breeding, hybrid crops
- **Forensics**: DNA fingerprinting
- **Evolution**: Natural selection

**Practice Problems:**
1. A heterozygous tall plant (Tt) is crossed with a homozygous short plant (tt). What are the offspring probabilities?
2. What is the difference between genotype and phenotype?
3. If both parents are carriers for cystic fibrosis (Cc), what is the chance their child will have the disease?
4. Why are pea plants good for genetics experiments?
5. How does independent assortment increase genetic variation?

---

### Lesson 09: Molecular Genetics

**Duration:** 75 minutes

**Hook**: "DNA is the blueprint of life. It contains instructions for building and operating every living thing."

**Story**: "In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick discovered the double helix structure of DNA. Their work built on X-ray images taken by Rosalind Franklin - without her data, they couldn't have solved the structure."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe DNA structure and replication
- Explain transcription and translation
- Understand the central dogma of biology
- Apply genetic principles to real problems

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Genetic testing, gene therapy
- **Forensics**: DNA fingerprinting
- **Agriculture**: Genetic modification
- **Evolution**: Comparing DNA across species

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the base pairing rules in DNA?
2. How does DNA replication occur?
3. What is the difference between transcription and translation?
4. How many codons code for amino acids?
5. What is a point mutation? Give an example.

---

### Lesson 10: Evolution Introduction

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Evolution explains the diversity of life. From bacteria to whales, all life is connected through common ancestors."

**Story**: "In 1859, Charles Darwin published 'On the Origin of Species.' He proposed that species evolve through natural selection - organisms with favorable traits survive and reproduce more successfully."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Define evolution and natural selection
- Understand evidence for evolution
- Describe mechanisms of evolution
- Apply evolutionary principles

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Antibiotic resistance
- **Agriculture**: Crop evolution, pest resistance
- **Conservation**: Biodiversity preservation
- **Forensics**: Evolutionary relationships

**Practice Problems:**
1. What are the four principles of natural selection?
2. Give 3 pieces of evidence for evolution.
3. How does antibiotic resistance evolve?
4. What is the difference between homologous and analogous structures?
5. How does evolution explain the diversity of finches in the Galápagos?
