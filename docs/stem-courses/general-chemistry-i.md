## Course Description

Atomic structure, periodic trends, chemical bonding, stoichiometry, and thermochemistry. This course connects fundamental chemistry to **everyday life, medicine, environmental science, and technology**. Students will learn through **molecular visualization, virtual labs, and real-world case studies**.

### Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students will be able to:
1. Describe atomic structure and periodic trends
2. Understand chemical bonding and molecular geometry
3. Perform stoichiometric calculations
4. Balance and predict chemical reactions
5. Apply thermochemistry principles

## Real-World Examples

### Medicine & Health

**Example 1: Drug Design**
Understanding molecular structure helps scientists design drugs that fit perfectly into protein targets - like a key in a lock. This is how antiviral medications are developed.

**Example 2: Blood pH**
Blood must maintain pH between 7.35 and 7.45. If it drops below 6.8 or rises above 7.8, you die. This is why understanding acids and bases is crucial in medicine.

### Environmental Science

**Example 3: Climate Change**
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) molecules absorb infrared radiation. Understanding molecular vibrations helps us predict how greenhouse gases affect climate.

**Example 4: Ozone Layer**
Ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere protects us from UV radiation. Understanding its formation and destruction helps us protect the ozone layer.

### Technology

**Example 5: Lithium-Ion Batteries**
Your phone's battery uses lithium ions moving between electrodes. Understanding electrochemistry helps us build better batteries.

**Example 6: Semiconductor Chips**
Computer chips are made from silicon with precise doping. Understanding atomic structure is essential for semiconductor technology.

### Everyday Life

**Example 7: Cooking**
Baking soda (NaHCO₃) reacts with vinegar (acetic acid) to produce CO₂ - that's why your cake rises!

**Example 8: Cleaning**
Bleach (sodium hypochlorite) works by oxidizing stains. Understanding redox reactions helps us use cleaning products safely.

## Story: The Accidental Discovery

> **Hook**: "Penicillin, Teflon, and X-rays were all discovered by accident. Chemistry is full of surprises."

> **Story**: In 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from vacation to find a petri dish contaminated with mold. Around the mold, bacteria couldn't grow. He had discovered penicillin - the first antibiotic. This accident saved millions of lives and won him the Nobel Prize.

> **Connection**: Chemistry isn't just about formulas - it's about curiosity and observation. The next great discovery could be yours.

## Lesson Structure

### Lesson 01: Matter and Measurement

**Duration:** 50 minutes

**Hook**: "Everything you see, touch, and taste is made of atoms. Chemistry is the study of how these atoms combine and rearrange."

**Story**: "In 1661, Robert Boyle published 'The Sceptical Chymist,' arguing that matter is made of atoms, not elements like earth, water, air, and fire. This was revolutionary - it challenged 2,000 years of thinking."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Classify matter as pure substances, mixtures, or solutions
- Convert between units using dimensional analysis
- Apply significant figures
- Use scientific notation

**Content Outline:**

#### 1. States of Matter (10 min)
- Solid, liquid, gas, plasma
- Changes of state
- Intermolecular forces preview

#### 2. Classification of Matter (10 min)
- Pure substances vs. mixtures
- Elements vs. compounds
- Heterogeneous vs. homogeneous mixtures

#### 3. Measurement (10 min)
- SI units
- Dimensional analysis
- Significant figures

#### 4. Scientific Notation (10 min)
- Writing large and small numbers
- Operations with scientific notation

#### 5. Interactive Elements (10 min)
- **Periodic Table Explorer**: Click on elements, see properties
- **Unit Converter**: Convert between measurement units
- **Significant Figures Calculator**: Practice rounding

**Real-World Application**: Medicine dosing - precise measurements save lives

**Practice Problems:**
1. Convert 5.5 km to meters
2. How many significant figures are in 0.00450?
3. Write 6.022 × 10²³ in standard notation
4. Classify each as element, compound, or mixture: water, air, gold, salt water
5. Calculate the density of a substance with mass 25.4 g and volume 12.0 mL

### Lesson 02: Atoms, Elements, and Compounds

**Duration:** 55 minutes

**Hook**: "There are 118 known elements, but just 6 make up 98% of all living things: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur."

**Story**: "Dmitri Mendeleev organized the periodic table in 1869. He left gaps for elements that hadn't been discovered yet - and predicted their properties. When gallium was discovered in 1875, it matched his predictions perfectly."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe atomic structure (protons, neutrons, electrons)
- Define atomic number, mass number, and isotopes
- Understand the periodic table organization
- Identify element properties from position

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Radioactive isotopes for imaging and treatment
- **Archaeology**: Carbon dating using isotopes
- **Technology**: Semiconductors, rare earth elements
- **Forensics**: Identifying elements at crime scenes

**Practice Problems:**
1. How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in ⁵⁶Fe?
2. What is the atomic number of an element with 24 protons?
3. What is the difference between ¹²C and ¹⁴C?
4. Which group contains the noble gases?
5. Why are transition metals good conductors?

### Lesson 03: Atomic Structure

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Quantum mechanics tells us that electrons don't orbit the nucleus like planets around the Sun. Instead, they exist in probability clouds - orbitals."

**Story**: "In 1927, Werner Heisenberg discovered the uncertainty principle: you cannot know both the exact position and exact momentum of an electron. This isn't a limitation of our instruments - it's a fundamental property of nature."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe the quantum mechanical model of the atom
- Understand electron configurations
- Interpret orbital diagrams
- Predict chemical properties from electron configuration

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Spectroscopy**: Identifying elements by their light emissions
- **Lasers**: Stimulated emission of light
- **LEDs**: Quantum dots for displays
- **MRI**: Nuclear magnetic resonance

**Practice Problems:**
1. Write the electron configuration for carbon (Z = 6).
2. Write the orbital diagram for oxygen.
3. What is the electron configuration of Fe²⁺?
4. Why do noble gases have full outer shells?
5. How does electron configuration relate to chemical reactivity?

---

### Lesson 04: Periodic Trends

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "The periodic table isn't just a chart - it's a roadmap. It tells you how elements will behave."

**Story**: "In 1869, Mendeleev arranged elements by atomic mass and noticed patterns. When he found gaps, he predicted undiscovered elements. His predictions were so accurate that the periodic table became the most important tool in chemistry."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Explain periodic trends (atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity)
- Predict element properties from position
- Compare elements within groups and periods
- Apply periodic trends to chemical behavior

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Materials Science**: Choosing elements for specific properties
- **Drug Design**: Understanding molecular interactions
- **Battery Technology**: Selecting electrode materials
- **Semiconductors**: Doping with specific elements

**Practice Problems:**
1. Which has the larger atomic radius: Na or Cl? Why?
2. Arrange Li, Na, K in order of increasing ionization energy.
3. Which is more electronegative: O or S? Why?
4. Why do alkali metals react vigorously with water?
5. Predict the formula of the compound formed between Ca and Cl.

### Lesson 05: Chemical Bonding

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Atoms bond to become stable - like people forming communities. The type of bond determines the properties of the substance."

**Story**: "In 1916, Gilbert Lewis proposed that atoms share electrons to form covalent bonds. His Lewis dot structures are still used today to predict molecular shapes and properties."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Describe ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding
- Draw Lewis structures
- Predict molecular geometry using VSEPR theory
- Identify bond polarity and molecular polarity

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Drug-receptor interactions
- **Materials**: Polymers, ceramics, metals
- **Biology**: Protein folding, DNA structure
- **Technology**: Semiconductors, conductors

**Practice Problems:**
1. Draw the Lewis structure for water.
2. What is the molecular geometry of NH₃?
3. Is CO₂ polar or nonpolar? Why?
4. What type of bonding is in NaCl? In H₂O? In copper?
5. Predict the bond angles in CH₄.

### Lesson 06: Ionic Compounds

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Table salt (NaCl) is ionic - sodium gives an electron to chlorine, creating ions that attract each other in a crystal lattice."

**Story**: "In 1913, William Bragg used X-rays to determine the structure of NaCl. He found that sodium and chlorine ions alternate in a perfect cube. This was the first time anyone 'saw' atoms."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Write formulas for ionic compounds
- Name ionic compounds
- Understand crystal lattice structures
- Calculate lattice energy

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Medicine**: Saline solutions, electrolyte balance
- **Food**: Preservatives, flavoring
- **Construction**: Cement, ceramics
- **Technology**: Solid-state batteries

**Practice Problems:**
1. Write the formula for aluminum oxide.
2. Name: MgCl₂, K₂O, FeCl₃.
3. What is the charge on iron in Fe₂O₃?
4. Draw a Lewis structure for NaCl showing electron transfer.
5. Why do ionic compounds have high melting points?

### Lesson 07: Covalent Bonding

**Duration:** 65 minutes

**Hook**: "Water, DNA, proteins, plastics - all held together by covalent bonds. These bonds are the foundation of life and modern materials."

**Story**: "In 1932, Linus Pauling published 'The Nature of the Chemical Bond,' explaining how atoms share electrons. He won the Nobel Prize and became one of the most influential chemists ever."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Draw Lewis structures for covalent molecules
- Predict molecular geometry (VSEPR)
- Understand hybridization
- Distinguish polar and nonpolar bonds

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Biology**: DNA base pairs, protein structure
- **Medicine**: Drug design, enzyme function
- **Materials**: Polymers, plastics, fibers
- **Technology**: Organic electronics

**Practice Problems:**
1. Draw the Lewis structure for methane (CH₄).
2. What is the hybridization of carbon in ethylene (C₂H₄)?
3. Is water bent or linear? Why?
4. Draw resonance structures for ozone (O₃).
5. Predict the shape of SF₆.

### Lesson 08: Stoichiometry

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Stoichiometry is the math of chemistry - it tells us how much of each substance we need and how much we'll produce."

**Story**: "In 1774, Antoine Lavoisier showed that mass is conserved in chemical reactions. This was the birth of stoichiometry. He weighed everything - even the air in his lab - and proved that matter is neither created nor destroyed."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand the mole concept
- Convert between mass, moles, and particles
- Balance chemical equations
- Perform stoichiometric calculations

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Pharmaceuticals**: Drug manufacturing
- **Industrial Chemistry**: Production of materials
- **Environmental Science**: Pollution calculations
- **Cooking**: Recipe scaling (stoichiometry of cooking!)

**Practice Problems:**
1. How many moles are in 36 g of water?
2. What is the mass of 2.5 moles of CO₂?
3. Balance: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
4. How many grams of water are produced from 10 g of H₂?
5. If 10 g of NaOH reacts with excess HCl, how many grams of NaCl are produced?

### Lesson 09: Chemical Reactions

**Duration:** 60 minutes

**Hook**: "Every time you cook, clean, or breathe, you're witnessing chemical reactions. Chemistry is the science of change."

**Story**: "Fire was humanity's first chemical reaction to control. Understanding combustion led to engines, power plants, and space rockets. The same reaction that warms your home also propels rockets to Mars."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Classify chemical reactions
- Predict products from reactants
- Balance chemical equations
- Understand reaction types

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Energy**: Combustion, fuel cells
- **Medicine**: Drug metabolism
- **Environmental**: Acid rain, ozone depletion
- **Industrial**: Manufacturing processes

**Practice Problems:**
1. Classify: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (what type?)
2. Predict products: Na + Cl₂ → ?
3. Balance: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
4. Write the net ionic equation for: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃
5. What type of reaction is: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂?

---

### Lesson 10: Thermochemistry

**Duration:** 70 minutes

**Hook**: "Every chemical reaction absorbs or releases energy. Understanding this helps us design better batteries, engines, and even understand how our bodies work."

**Story**: "In 1843, James Joule showed that mechanical work could be converted to heat. He stirred water with a falling weight and measured the temperature rise. This proved energy conservation and gave us the joule - the unit of energy."

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand enthalpy changes
- Apply Hess's law
- Calculate heat transfer
- Distinguish exothermic and endothermic reactions

**Real-World Applications:**
- **Energy**: Power plants, batteries
- **Medicine**: Metabolism, body temperature
- **Environmental**: Climate change, global warming
- **Cooking**: Heat transfer in cooking

**Practice Problems:**
1. Is the reaction CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O exothermic or endothermic?
2. Calculate the enthalpy change for: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (ΔH = -572 kJ)
3. Use Hess's law to find ΔH for: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
4. How much heat is released when 5 g of methane burns?
5. Why does your body feel hot when exercising?
